## Premise

A trading platform loses three kinds of thing for good, and everything else it
computes can be computed again.

1. **Captured market data.** A quote not recorded when it existed is gone. A
   vendor's history is no substitute, because vendors restate history after
   the fact. The sibling
   [quantitative-trading](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading)
   repository measured that drift: an adjusted close is a function of the day
   it was downloaded.
2. **The order and fill record.** It is the audit trail, the input that
   calibrates the transaction cost model, and the only way to reconcile what
   the platform thinks it holds against what the broker says it holds. A fill
   nobody journaled cannot be reconstructed from the platform's own state.
3. **Capital.** A strategy can be re-researched. A loss cannot be undone.

A forecast, a backtest, a target portfolio and a report are all regenerable
from those three. That asymmetry is what the ranking rule in
[CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md) protects. A path that can lose captured data or the
order record is never deferred, and a path that can lose capital sits behind
the hard limits, which no ranking overrides.

## One runtime

What is tested must be what runs live. A backtester written apart from the
live engine is a second implementation of every strategy it runs, and the two
drift without either looking wrong. Narang names backtest software and data
feed handlers among the places model risk lives (L4616 in the
[highlights](https://github.com/l3a0/trading-strategies/blob/main/research/book-notes/inside-the-black-box.md)).

Kleppmann reaches the same conclusion about data pipelines. Keeping one piece
of logic in both a batch system and a streaming system costs significant
effort, and debugging, tuning and maintaining two systems stays costly even
when a library lets one piece of code target both (*Designing
Data-Intensive Applications*, 1st ed., O'Reilly Media, 2017, Kindle location
15718, cited below as DDIA L15718).

So the platform has one set of modules, following the boxes in the charter's
module table, and three adapters at its edges.

1. **The data source**, which replays committed history in a backtest and
   reads a live feed on paper and live.
2. **The clock**, which steps through history in a backtest and follows the
   wall clock otherwise.
3. **The broker**, which is a simulator in a backtest and in shadow running, a
   broker's paper account on paper, and the real account only once the owner
   switches it.

The price is that every module has to be written against an event and clock
interface from its first commit, which is slower than a vectorised backtest
over a price array. What it buys is that a strategy's backtest and its paper
run exercise the same lines of code.

## Design principles

A principle earns a place here only when a design can pass or fail it, which is the test *The Art of Scalability* sets for its own (Abbott and Fisher, 1st ed., Addison-Wesley, 2009, Kindle location 4588, cited below as AoS L4588). A principle like "scales infinitely" fails that test, because no design can be checked against it. Each rule below names the check a review runs, and the reason for it.

The owner's standing directive, adopted on 2026-11-03, is Jeff Dean's rule as *Building Microservices* quotes it: design for 10 times the first load, plan to rewrite before 100 times, and build for the first load (BM L4428, cited below). A design passes when reaching 10 times takes configuration and more machines, and it writes down, for growth beyond that, the load at which planning a rewrite starts, what the rewrite would replace, and what must survive it unchanged, which here is captured data, the journal, their formats and the partition layout, and the tests of the risk gate's refusals, the halts and the kill switch. Two alarms are built at the first load. One trips at 75% of each written scale limit in the running system, beside the test of rule 2 of [Design ahead](#design-ahead-and-write-down-where-the-build-stops). The other trips when measured load on a named load parameter, read on its busiest key, reaches the level written for planning the rewrite, and pages the owner through the alert path once per parameter and level, naming both, and the owner files the issue. The page is recorded where a change of release cannot lose it, it reports under the first monitors' heartbeat, and a test feeds load at the level and asserts one page, then starts a second session above the level and asserts none. The level sits below the most that configuration alone reaches, with a stated lead time. The architect writes it beside the scale limits, and the operator runs the alarm. The book states the same idea as designing every major system so it can be split along at least two axes, while building only the split that today's load needs (AoS L4684, L8205). Its three axes are clones of the whole system (x), a split by function or by kind of data (y), and a split by a key such as account or symbol (z) (AoS L7432).

*Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, cited in [One runtime](#one-runtime), adds the rules for data that the first book leaves out: what to do about faults, clocks, retries, concurrent writers and records that must outlive every bug. A citation like DDIA L9286 is the Kindle location of one of the owner's highlights or of a screen of the book's full text. One like DDIA L\~14160 points into the full text at a location good to about one screen, because the reader shows this book's Kindle pages rather than its locations, so the location of a screen with no highlight on it is interpolated. Where its rules restate one already here, the rule below cites both books rather than appearing twice.

*Code Complete* adds the rules for the code itself, which both architecture books leave out (McConnell, 2nd ed., Microsoft Press, 2004). A citation like CC p. 197 is a printed page of that edition. Its case for writing each rule as a check rather than a habit is measured: no single way of finding defects reaches more than about 75% at its typical rate, unit and integration testing reach about 30 to 35%, and two methods combined find nearly twice what one finds (CC p. 470, p. 471). So each of its rules below names the tool, test or review that checks it.

Sam Newman's *Building Microservices* (O'Reilly Media, 2015) adds the rules for where modules are cut, how a release reaches each environment, and how the running loop is traced and watched. A citation like BM L4252 is the Kindle location of one of the owner's highlights or of a screen of the book's full text, and BM L\~932 marks a location good to about one screen, as for DDIA. The book argues for splitting a system into services, and its own author's team found splitting too soon costly. The owner ruled on 2026-10-29, on [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7#issuecomment-6505752788), that each component runs as its own process and passes its output to the others through files. So the book's rules apply here to the boundaries between those components, to splitting a component further, to releases, to tracing and watching the running loop, and to the handling of each dependency.

Viktor Farcic's *The DevOps 2.0 Toolkit* (2016) adds the rules for checking a release where it runs, building machines from code, healing a running system without hiding a fault, and gathering its logs. A citation like DT p. 247 is a printed page of the book's PDF edition. Its tools, such as Vagrant, Ansible, Docker Swarm, Consul, nginx and Jenkins, are the book's examples rather than choices made here.

So these rules are checks on a design, and a review applies all of them. 3. The guards on the order path, whose price is paid by building them late: the sizing rule, the account's lease with its tag check before every broker call, the lease number in every journal entry and client order id and its continuation in a lease store created again, the decision's id, or an override entry's, a contract-suite run's or a close entry's, in every record and client order id, applying each fill once, the block on orders in doubt, the halt state read just before each send, and the gate's refusal of an order that adds to a position whose worst loss it cannot compute. The checks in CI that hold the rules for the code itself, such as the import rule test, strict linting and type checking, and the run under `python -O`, belong here too, because strictness switched on late leaves a backlog of code written to a looser standard.

### Design ahead, and write down where the build stops

1. **The next scale step exists as a written design, and the only code built ahead of it is configuration.** The book's example is a data-access layer designed so it could reach several databases while one runs. Its migration scripts are written when the need arises, and the design is what lets a second database take weeks rather than months (AoS L1078). Here the only code built ahead is a configuration value, such as the map of partitions to machines, whose other settings a test runs. A script or path offered later as the next step has run once, in a drill on a copy, before anything relies on it.
2. **Every implementation states its written scale limit, and an alarm or a test trips at 75% of it.** Code built for "any number of read nodes" that handles five has a limit of five (AoS L1174). The limit is stated beside the code that enforces it, and 75% is the headroom ceiling in rule 4. On the order path, reaching the limit halts.
3. **A partition key and a fixed partition count are set from the first commit.** The book finds a split by function "not so easy" to design room for in advance (AoS L7781), so module boundaries and data ownership are settled early. Captured data is keyed by its source, the vendor's identifier as received, and the date it was recorded. The key only routes a record and never identifies it, and a write into a key that already holds data adds a new vintage rather than replacing one. The trading loop is keyed by account. Only the identifier part of a key, the source and vendor identifier or the account, maps to a partition through a hash, and the date orders records inside the partition, since hashing scatters keys that a range read wants together (DDIA L6235). That hash is the same in every process. DDIA warns that a language's built-in hash can differ from one process to the next, naming Java's and Ruby's (DDIA L6211), and Python's `hash()` on a string does the same. AoS treats the count of a split by key as a number to raise later (AoS L7781). DDIA finds that changing the count moves data, so a design built on fixed partitions sets the count when the data is first laid out and grows by moving whole partitions between machines (DDIA L6411, L6443). Routing by the key modulo the machine count moves most keys whenever a machine is added (DDIA L6418). So the count is fixed at the start, set well above the machine count and chosen for 100 times the first load, the ceiling a rewrite must come before, because the partition layout is part of what must survive a rewrite and changing the count moves captured data, and no higher, since each partition carries its own overhead (DDIA L6443).
4. **Plan headroom per component, at 50% of measured capacity and never above 75%.** The measured maximum can be wrong, growth estimates can be wrong, and behaviour near the limit turns erratic (AoS L4449, L4475, L4480). Headroom is computed for each component, never for the system as a whole (AoS L4496). Here that means capture disk, journal volume and the broker's API rate limits. A share of the rate limit is reserved for cancels, the kill switch's among them, and new orders and queries never draw on it.
5. **Every written scale limit names the load parameter it is measured in, and the design names the operations it assumes are common.** DDIA's case is that a scalable architecture rests on assumptions about which operations are common and which are rare, and that a wrong assumption makes the scaling work wasted at best and harmful at worst (DDIA L627). It also finds that a fast-growing service usually rethinks its architecture at each tenfold rise in load (DDIA L598). That finding matches the owner's directive, which expects a rewrite before 100 times. A design for 10 times the first load holds only along the parameters it names, so it names them, such as orders per second per account, symbols captured, and messages per second at the open, and a review checks them against measured use. Load along a parameter the design never named is the case that forces a rewrite. Stateful parts stay on one machine until cost or an availability target forces a split, which DDIA reports as the common practice for databases until recently (DDIA L614). The same chapter finds that an early product does better to iterate on its features than to scale for load it may never see (DDIA L621), which is the build-at-1x half of the directive. A written limit holds for the heaviest key, not the average. DDIA's case is a service whose users average about 75 followers while one user's post reaches over 30 million, so the spread of followers per user was the load parameter that mattered (DDIA L464, L473). Here that means the busiest symbol at the open, and the largest partition of any partitioned job, which finishes only when its slowest partition does (DDIA L12589).

### Keep the order path isolated

The **order path** runs from the target portfolio through the risk gate and the journal to the broker adapter. A **synchronous dependency** on it is a call that leaves the process or waits on input or output. A call between modules inside the process, through their shared interfaces, is not one.

1. **Nothing that does not earn money can stall the order path.** The book's first rule of fault isolation is to protect whatever is closest to the money (AoS L7130). Monitoring, reports, dashboards and research reach the order path only asynchronously, and are allowed to fail. The book's own case is a monitoring database whose slowness backed up every user query until the site went down (AoS L8616). The loss stop and the kill switch live in the risk gate and are read on every order, never in monitoring.
2. **Count the synchronous dependencies on the order path and keep them to the minimum.** Availability multiplies along a chain. One hundred parts at 99.999% each give about 99.9% together, which turns about 5 minutes of downtime a year into over 500 (AoS L8698). The minimum here is the input data, the risk gate, the account's lease, the journal and the broker, and for a close the owner confirmed, the owner's record. The journal write is synchronous on purpose, because hard limit 4 puts it before the send.
3. **A fault boundary is drawn around the whole loop, never around a layer, and every crossing is listed.** In the book, a unit isolated from faults shares nothing with its neighbours, takes no calls across its boundary, and carries every transaction from start to finish inside itself (AoS L7088, L7101, L7113). A boundary drawn around all the databases, or all the risk checks, only groups like parts, and one failure there still reaches every transaction (AoS L7117). The book lets one boundary hold one tenant or many (AoS L7147). So the first build is one boundary holding every strategy sleeve, and a split by account comes when load or risk calls for it. Two strategies in one broker account share buying power, so they never sit in different boundaries. Three crossings are allowed, and any other breaks the boundary.
   1. Captured data flows in, read only and one way, and a stale feed halts the sleeves that read it.
   2. Monitoring, and the copy of recorded data to its store off the machine, flow outward only, apart from the read at a start that crossing 3 names.
   3. The kill switch, and the time reference the clock-offset halt reads, flow inward to every boundary, and a boundary that cannot read either halts. A start's read of a copy of the journal flows inward once, and rule 3 of [A release is checked where it runs](#a-release-is-checked-where-it-runs-and-nothing-heals-by-hiding-a-fault) says what a start that cannot finish it serves. A confirmation of an outside entry flows inward as a record only the owner's role can write, which each reader checks itself.
4. **The risk gate holds every new order to the same limits, whatever sent it, and only the execution worker holds a credential that can send.** Hard limit 2 already sends every order through the gate. Newman's confused deputy adds the reason no caller may carry its own authority past it: a service tricked into using its authority on behalf of a caller who should not have it (BM L3634). So no caller carries a flag, a role or a credential that lets its orders skip a limit. A strategy's orders and an operator command's are new orders held to every limit, and a replay runs against the simulated broker. The loss stop does not unwind in slice 1, under the owner's 2026-10-29 ruling on [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7), and an unwind, once [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) adds one, is a new order held to every limit. So is the close after a broker's trade under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control), apart from the stops that case names, which the gate decides from the close entry and no caller carries. Replay, backtest and shadow hold no broker credential, and the paper and live accounts each have their own, as the owner's 2026-10-29 ruling on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) keeps the broker key readable by the execution worker's user alone. The kill switch's cancels, a halt's cancels and any cancel the gate classifies as reducing exposure are exempt from the limits and the input checks, and from nothing else. A cancel of a protective order that no halt's list and no kill switch names is held to the limits, because it raises exposure. Every cancel waits for its journal entry's acknowledged write, as an order does, and is sent by the lease's holder and drawn from the rate-limit share kept for cancels. A replacement is a new order held to every limit, sent in the sequence [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) rules. Rule 4 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) says which cancels raise exposure.

### Modules are cut by capability, and a component splits further only with evidence

1. **Each module is cut along a capability of the business, its published output is its interface, and no other module reads its internal state.** Newman draws boundaries along bounded contexts, the parts of a domain that keep their internal models to themselves (BM L740), and finds that services sharing one database lose both cohesion and loose coupling (BM L\~932). Here the modules are the charter's module table, and under the owner's 2026-10-29 ruling each passes its output to the others as files. A module's published files, in their versioned formats, are its interface, and anything else it stores is its own. The journal is appended only through its own writer and read only through its reader, wherever [#22](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/22) places them. Backups, the scheduled read-back and replay take recorded files whole, which is a copy rather than a read of a module's records, and any parse of a copied file goes through the owning module's reader, from wherever [#22](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/22)'s package diagram places it. The import rule test that [#22](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/22) specifies holds the code side. A cut along a technical layer, such as one that only wraps storage, needs a named reason, such as a performance goal, because the book makes the technical seam the second driver, never the first (BM L866).
2. **A component is cut into new components only with a named reason and the history of changes across its boundary.** Newman's own team split a product into services early, merged them back after a few months, and split it again a year later along boundaries that held, and he finds splitting too soon costly, above all in a domain the team is new to (BM L777, BM L796). So a proposal to cut a component into new ones with a new interface between them names the load, isolation or credential that calls for it and shows how often the component's interface changed. Clones of a component and splits by key, such as competing execution instances or a partition by account, follow [Design ahead](#design-ahead-and-write-down-where-the-build-stops) and need no such proposal, and neither does a split the owner has already ruled on.

### Faults are expected, and the platform checks itself while it runs

1. **Every fault the design says it handles is injected on purpose, in replay.** DDIA separates a fault, one part leaving its specification, from a failure, the whole system no longer serving (DDIA L293). It finds many critical bugs in poor error handling, and recommends causing faults on purpose to keep that handling exercised, and testing even unlikely ones (DDIA L300, L8461). This doc reads "poor" as handling that rarely runs. Here the simulated broker and the replay harness can inject twenty-two faults, the harness working on its own copy of every store and record it injects on, and a test runs each through the full loop and fails when the handling is removed. A fault a later change handles, such as those [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) proposes or [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks for, joins this list as it is built. The halt on a difference from the broker joins it when the tests in [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) item 8 are built.
   1. An acknowledgment of an order or of a cancel that never arrives, and one that arrives late. For a cancel the test checks each branch of rule 6: a lookup that settles the cancel, a resend journaled first, resends at one below the written number, at it and at one above, a run from another account, either way, opening no resend, a kill switch's repeat going out with no run and held back when the lookup shows a cancel pending and once the written number is used up, for every other outcome, a halt's or a decision's cancel with no run among them, no resend, and, wherever the cancel stays unsettled, a resend included, a halt and a page past the written time counted from the first entry, its reason read back from the halt-state monitor, and a cancel still passing during the halt.
   2. A fill delivered twice, a fill that arrives after a cancel was acknowledged, a fill the broker later corrects or busts, and a second message under one execution id that differs from the first.
   3. A process killed between the journal write and the broker call, and killed in the middle of a write to a local file.
   4. A lease holder paused after its tag check and before its request leaves, past the end of its lease.
   5. A clock that jumps forward or back, and a time service that stops answering.
   6. A feed that stops, a feed that repeats messages, and a feed that skips sequence numbers.
   7. A local disk write that blocks for seconds under capture's spill.
   8. A journal that cannot be read, which the procedure for orders in doubt in [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer) settles.
   9. A derived view that lags by minutes to hours, and an off-machine copy that stops (DDIA L5126).
   10. A second execution instance taking the account's lease while the first still runs.
   11. A reporting dependency that blocks, such as the log pipeline, while orders still go out on time, where every dropped line is counted, the fill alarm fires at 75% of the bound and before the first drop, and a scheduled drift check that stops running alerts.
   12. A dependency that recovers while its breaker is open, whose recovery probe sends no order.
   13. A job off the order path that crashes its worker on every attempt, set aside at its retry maximum with an alert, tested one attempt below the maximum, at it and one above.
   14. A consumer of broker messages that crashes on one message, which halts on the first crash with the message named and journaled.
   15. A broker or store call that stalls until its breaker opens, while a cancel and the kill switch still go out from the slice of its limit kept for them.
   16. A store that is unreachable, where no cancel is sent and the page names the owner's step in the broker's own interface.
   17. A dependency that fails again and again through a session, whose breaker's halt releases itself up to its written count, tested one release below the count, at it and one above, and on a live account waits for the owner from the first.
   18. Once a step that reads a metric first runs, a null, stale or failed reading behind it. A release record or restart switch that cannot be read, and a switch flipped off, each followed by a crash, where a component the order path depends on halts trading with its last exit and count written, and pages the owner, and any other component pages. Execution, with its switch or count unreadable or absent, restarts, comes up halted, serves cancels and waits for the owner. With its switch flipped off it restarts halted, serves cancels, waits for the owner and pages, naming the broker's interface only when that restart fails. With its record unreadable it restarts, serves nothing and pages naming the broker's interface. Capture keeps restarting once this platform runs it. A restart or scaling switch found off at a session's start alerts.
   19. A component that halts and is left running, which no health check restarts and whose restart count does not move, a component restarted while halted, which comes up halted, and a consumer of a journal entry or captured data that crashes on one record, which halts on the first crash with the record named and takes no second restart into it.
   20. Execution restarted one restart below its cap and at it, which comes up trading, and one above, which comes up halted and still serves cancels.
   21. A store of the journal, captured data, their backups, the halt state, the lease or execution's queue that goes missing or is created again, a journal or captured-data store found empty or behind its copies, and a lease store created again whose lease number continues, each of which halts new orders and never reads as empty, while capture, once this platform runs it, keeps recording. A journal store found empty or behind takes no entry until it is restored, a journal verified to a non-empty head whose comparison with a copy cannot finish halts new orders and serves cancels, a copy reporting a position no entry backs counts as that comparison, an empty store with its first-start record absent, unreadable or spent halts, a store that comes back leaves its halt until the owner releases it, and a missing halt-state store's reason reaches the owner on the page.
   22. Once a scaling step first runs, an instance removed by scaling or rescheduling with a broker call open, whose order stays in doubt and is never resent, a scale-down refused at execution's floor and allowed one above it, the floor's alarm silent at it and firing one below it, and a scale step tested one below the written scale limit, at it and one above.
2. **A guarantee the platform relies on is checked while it runs, and a mismatch halts.** DDIA's example is a queue that compares messages in with messages out and alerts on any gap (DDIA L372). Faults that share a cause across parts produce more failures than independent hardware faults, and the bugs behind them wait for the day an assumption stops holding (DDIA L351, L366). So the checks run on every fill and at a set interval, not only at the end of the day: the journal's orders against the broker's, fills against positions, and the vendor's sequence numbers against what capture recorded. A check that sees two moments reports a gap that is only timing, and a platform that halts on its own timing learns to ignore the alarm (DDIA L7218, L7227). The journal and the broker share no snapshot, so reconciliation records the journal position it read, reads the broker after it, and treats a difference explained only by entries journaled after that position as in flight, to recheck on the next run. Any other difference halts at once, with no tolerance on quantity and no count of repeats. An order, fill or holding change at the broker with no journal entry, such as the owner's own trade, a split or an assignment, halts trading. A halt on a difference from the broker reads every working and in-doubt order against the position the broker reports. An order whose fill would move that position away from its decision's target, or past it, no longer serves that decision, so the halt cancels it, journaled first and handled as rule 3 of [The code holds its own rules](#the-code-holds-its-own-rules-and-every-check-runs-in-production) and rule 6 below say. A sell sent to close a long the broker has since closed is the plain case, because its fill would open a short no decision asked for. Each decision's target is journaled before its first order, so this read has a record to use. An order this test cannot judge stays, and a missing target never reads as zero. That covers a target that is absent or unreadable, a position read that is absent, failed or empty, or that omits the instrument, unless the broker's contract suite has a case showing that answer means flat, an order in doubt the lookup cannot map, a difference on an instrument no strategy holds or that more than one strategy holds, and a split whose two records rule 6 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) compares do not yet agree. Once they agree, execution journals the split, then the book and the decision's target restated in the new shares as new entries, runs the test in those shares and journals its cancels. The halt cannot be released until that is done, and where any of it cannot finish, the halt stays and the page says why. Every other order stays too, and the cancel that keeps a pair whole still goes out. No person trades at the broker in the platform's account, so an order that traces to no decision, override, contract-suite run, close entry, broker's own record or owner's confirmation falls under the halt in rule 3 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) on an order that traces to nothing, which cancels it where it adds to a position and keeps it where it reduces one. An override has no decision's target, so it stays. Once a close entry under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) sets a leg's target to flat, flat is the target this test reads, for the close's own order too. Where rule 3 also halts on the same event, its cancel of adding orders applies as well, and an order either rule cancels is cancelled. The page names each order the halt cancelled, each it kept and why, and each position left off its target. [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) how the read counts later fills and several working orders. The price is twofold. A cancel lands only before the broker's cutoff, so a difference seen after it leaves the order working, and a position left off its target stays there until a person acts, because this halt sends no new order, apart from the close after a broker's trade under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control). Execution reconciles, because reconciling changes the book. Monitoring compares too, and never changes the book. Execution may journal a split, or a trade the broker made itself, such as a forced close, a buy-in or an assignment, as an outside entry from the broker's own record, assigned to the one strategy that holds the instrument, which releases the halt once the entry explains the whole difference, any close entry case 3 of rule 5 needs exists, and every leg has its outcome and its recorded page with its order settled and stopped working, though in live trading only the owner clears it, as the owner ruled on [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11#issuecomment-6529517074). For a split, rule 6 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) also requires the two records to agree. A record counts as a split only under a type the broker's contract suite has a case for, and as such a trade only under a type that itself marks the trade as the broker's own, with a case resting on a recorded response from that broker, because a case written from published text is a named gap. Any other, absent or unrecognised type waits for the owner like anything else, an ordinary fill included. A trade the broker made leaves its strategy off under rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it), because the next decision would otherwise buy the change back, and that entry can start the close of what the broker left, under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control). Anything else waits for the owner to confirm its source and its strategy, since nothing the platform holds separates a person's trade from a second sender's. An entry the owner confirms this way leaves its strategy off under rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it). One the owner confirms as the broker's own trade can also start that close, because a person has then said what the record does not. Queries to the broker draw on the general share of its rate limit, never on the share reserved for cancels.
3. **Every recorded record carries a checksum, and every read verifies it.** The checksums built into network protocols catch corruption on the wire, but not a bug at either end or corruption on disk, so DDIA calls for checksums from end to end (DDIA L16445, L9376). A crash can also leave a half-written record at the end of an append-only file, and a checksum is how a reader tells it from a whole one (DDIA L2471). DDIA's example detects such a record and ignores it. The journal's store writes each entry whole, as an object either exists or does not, so a torn journal entry cannot occur there. A local file can tear. A torn tail on a capture file is sealed and flagged, its bytes are moved aside and kept, and capture starts a new segment, which never halts capture. A record that fails its checksum anywhere else is corruption, and the reader halts and alerts. Recorded data nobody reads is still read back on a schedule, its checksums verified and its copies compared, because data that is only checked when read can rot unseen (DDIA L5489, L\~16792). The read-back is a rolling cycle that reads every segment once within a stated number of days, at low disk priority or against the off-machine copy, and a segment planted with a wrong checksum shows on each cycle that it can fire. A dead-man check running off the machine watches the heartbeat, the read-back's last cycle, the age of the newest off-machine copy, the last run of each strategy's outcome match and retiring check, the last run of the daily report, the last run of the check of orders back to their causes, and the age of the scheduled drift check's last result.
4. **A case the design calls impossible ends in a halt and an alert, never in a guess.** Real code still has to handle what an algorithm assumes cannot happen, even when the handling is to stop and leave the cleanup to a person (DDIA L9488). DDIA divides properties into safety, meaning nothing bad happens, which must hold in every case, and liveness, meaning something good eventually happens, which may wait on conditions (DDIA L9454, L9466, L9469). Here every hard limit is a safety property and trading is a liveness property. A design may give up trading to keep a hard limit, and never the reverse. A safety property holds within a stated model of what can go wrong, and DDIA calls a model that bounds every pause unrealistic (DDIA L9405, L9466), so no hard limit here depends on timing. A cancel reduces risk, so it is not trading under this rule, though cancelling a resting protective order or an unwind does raise exposure, so every halt and the kill switch say which orders they cancel and which they keep. The kill switch acts as a lease holder, so it is never a second sender. It runs in whichever instance receives its event, as the owner ruled on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10), takes the account's lease, waiting for a hung holder's lease to expire if it must, journals its cancels, sends them from the rate-limit share reserved for cancels, and reads the broker's open orders again until a read returns none, and an order still open whose cancel has no answer is handled under rule 6 below. Each halt states whether it releases itself after reconciliation and a hold time, or waits for the owner, and writes its reason where the monitor of the halt state reads it. On a live account no halt releases itself, and only the owner releases one, a fired loss stop and the kill switch among them. On paper and shadow the operator may too, under the owner's rulings on [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11). The owner counts as unreachable when a page stays unacknowledged past a stated time.
5. **A value from outside the process is checked against a sane range and a size limit when it is parsed, and a value that fails halts its consumer.** DDIA recommends such checks even between services that trust each other (DDIA L9360). The sane range comes from each instrument's metadata, since some instruments trade at negative prices, and a quantity above the instrument's size limit is the kind of value that stops the reader before it reaches a decision.
6. **Every outside call has a timeout, the order path's dependencies each have a limit on concurrent calls and a breaker, and an open breaker stops new orders and never a cancel the gate exempts.** Newman puts a timeout on every outside call, and names bulkheads and circuit breakers as tools to use where they fit (BM L4252, BM L4292, BM L4995). A breaker stops calling a dependency after a written number of failures and probes it until it answers. This design gives a limit and a breaker to the order path's dependencies: the feed, the broker, and the store that holds the lease and the journal, which share one breaker. The read of an owner's record, under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control), has a limit and a breaker of its own, and an open one holds that close as unreadable state. Every other dependency, such as the time servers, the off-machine copy and reporting, has a timeout and stays asynchronous, the copy apart from the read at a start that crossing 3 of [Keep the order path isolated](#keep-the-order-path-isolated) names, under rule 1 of [Keep the order path isolated](#keep-the-order-path-isolated). His cautionary tale is a pricing queue with no retry limit, where one request that crashed its worker went back on the queue and crashed the next (BM L1286). An open breaker on the feed, the broker or the store halts new orders under hard limit 3 rather than serving a stale value, names the breaker where the halt-state monitor reads it, and pages the owner. A cancel and the kill switch pass through an open breaker with every call they need: the lease's take, renewal, tag check and delete, the journal's re-read and write, the halt-state read, and the broker lookups below. Each order-path dependency keeps a written slice of its limit on concurrent calls for those calls, so a stalled new-order call cannot hold a cancel back. A cancel whose answer is lost is looked up first. When the lookup shows the order still working and no cancel pending, a new cancel goes through the gate's classification again and goes out, journaled first and sent under the lease, at most a written number of times for each cancel, where the contract suite's latest scheduled run against that account shows the broker applies a repeated cancel at most once, under the owner's 2026-11-03 ruling on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10). A lookup that shows the order cancelled, filled or expired settles the cancel and is journaled as received. Every other outcome leaves the cancel in doubt. That covers a lookup that cannot answer or shows a cancel pending, a written number already used up, a contract case that account's run cannot provoke, and a scheduled run that is missing, stale or failed. A resend does not end the doubt until a lookup or an answer shows the order cancelled, filled or expired, so a broker's acceptance of a cancel leaves it in doubt. The clock runs from the first cancel's journal entry, so a resend never resets it, and any cancel still unsettled past a written time halts new orders and pages the owner, under the owner's ruling on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10). That halt names each unsettled cancel by client order id, with its age, where the halt-state monitor reads it. It cancels nothing further, keeps every working order, persists between sessions and waits for the owner, and its page names the owner's step in the broker's own interface. A halt still stops no cancel. A run counts only for the account it ran against, so paper and live stay independent, and until a run exists for a live account every lost cancel there that no lookup or answer settles ends in that halt. The kill switch alone needs no run, under the owner's ruling on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10): its repeat keeps the lookup and the written number, at paper and at live. An answer such as "already filled" is journaled as received. An order in doubt is first looked up by client order id and cancelled once the lookup maps it. The kill switch's reads and the lookups draw on the general share of the rate limit, as every query does under rule 2, and the share reserved for cancels carries their cancels. When the store itself is down, no cancel can be journaled, so none is sent, as the owner ruled on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10): the page says so, and the owner's step is to cancel in the broker's own interface, which reconciliation later journals as outside entries once the owner confirms them. The call that tests whether a dependency recovered is a read, never an order. A breaker that closes ends only its own state, and the halt it raised releases under rule 4 above, after reconciliation from a recorded journal position, its hold time and no order or cancel left in doubt, at most a written number of times a session, each release journaled with its count, after which it waits for the owner and names the count. In live trading it waits for the owner from the first, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [PR #31](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/31), and the price is that a dependency's outage stops Live until the owner answers. Capture's connection to the feed sits outside the trading loop's breaker, and capture reconnects with bounded backoff and never stops retrying. A retried job off the order path, such as a report or a derived view, has a maximum, past which it is set aside and alerts. A job that consumes broker messages, the journal or captured data halts with the message named, and an order send is never retried, since a send with no answer stays in doubt under the one-writer rules.

### Time comes from one clock, and order from sequence numbers

1. **Elapsed time is measured on a monotonic clock, and the journal orders its entries by a sequence number it assigns.** The time-of-day clock can jump backward when the time service resets it, and a paused virtual machine sees time jump forward (DDIA L8811, L8823, L8878). A counter orders events without measuring time, which DDIA calls the safer way to order them (DDIA L8966, L10533), when one assigner increments it. Counters kept by several nodes, or handed out in blocks, do not agree with the order events happened in (DDIA L10556). So each account's journal numbers its entries from one counter, the numbers have no gaps, a gap halts the reader, and numbers from two accounts are never compared, since order holds only within one partition (DDIA L10731, L6201). The shared clock offers both readings, every timeout reads the monotonic one, a limit on a wait that must outlast a process is measured between journal stamps with the clock's bound added so it ends sooner, as the close's wait after a broker's trade is, and a timestamp in the journal records when something happened, never in what order. A monotonic clock does not defend against a pause, which can fall between a check and the act it guards (DDIA L9084).
2. **The machine's offset from reference time and the age of its last sync are readings of the clock, and either past its bound halts trading.** A computer's quartz clock drifts, a blocked time service can go unnoticed, and a time server can be wrong by hours (DDIA L8846, L8853, L8859). DDIA's rule for software that relies on synchronized clocks is to monitor the offset and take a node whose clock has drifted too far out of service (DDIA L8913). Here the clock sets session boundaries, data freshness and every captured timestamp, so its offset is one of the inputs hard limit 3 halts on. A time client that cannot reach its servers keeps reporting its last offset while the real error grows, so trading also halts when the last successful sync is older than a set age, and the offset is measured against several servers, since one can be wrong by hours (DDIA L8851, L9360). The offset and the sync age are readings of the clock adapter, so the backtest clock supplies them from the replay and one halt rule reads them in every environment. Each measurement is recorded as captured data, so receive times taken while the clock drifted can be found later. The bound sits well below the smallest staleness threshold, and a staleness check on event time adds the bound to the age it measures, since a clock running behind makes stale data read as fresh. A drill on the paper host blocks the time servers and shows both halts fire. The 100-microsecond rule DDIA cites as a draft for high-frequency firms (DDIA L8887) is far tighter than a platform this slow needs.
3. **Captured data keeps the source's event time, the source's send time where the feed has one, and the local receive time side by side, and rate monitors window by event time.** Mixing event time with processing time makes a backlog processed after a restart look like a burst of activity (DDIA L14747). A clock outside the platform's control can be wrong, so DDIA logs three timestamps, the event and send times on the sender's clock and the receive time on the receiver's, and estimates the offset between the clocks from the send and receive times (DDIA L14784, L14788). None of them overwrites another. Capture also gives every record its own receive sequence, gap-free and in receive order, since vendor sequence numbers are per feed and the clock can step back. The [charter's premise](team-charter.md#the-premise-what-must-not-fail) records when data arrived and when it became knowable. Receive time is when it arrived. **Knowable time** is the earliest moment the platform held the value: the receive time for live capture, and the vendor's publication date or vintage for history. Event time is neither, since a quarter's earnings carry the quarter's end as their event time and become knowable weeks later (L3884). The point-in-time interface reads by knowable time, and event time serves rate monitors.
4. **A decision reads the time and random numbers only from the shared clock and a seed the journal records, and it depends on nothing else that can change between runs.** A replica that re-runs a statement calling the current time or a random number computes a different result (DDIA L4912), and a replay does the same. DDIA's full list adds iterating over a hash table and reading from an outside source (DDIA L13148). So no module calls the system clock or an unseeded random source directly, iterates a set of strings, or anything built in such a set's order, where the order affects the result, or fetches from outside mid-decision. A Python dictionary keeps its insertion order, so it is not on that list. Each decision's journal entry also names the version of the reference data it used: risk limits, instrument metadata, configuration and the cost model. DDIA's version of that rule gives each version of a changing reference record its own identifier and joins against the version current at the time (DDIA L14943).
5. **Replay delivers events in the order live capture received them, and never re-sorts them by event time.** A node that must decide now cannot know which earlier-stamped event is still on its way, so ordering by timestamp finds the answer only after everything has arrived (DDIA L10603). A backtest that sorts a day by event time decides with hindsight no live run had. Replay orders by capture's receive sequence. Whatever wait for late events the live loop applies, replay applies the same one. A test replays a recording whose event times are out of order and asserts delivery in receive order, and a second test replays one day twice in separate processes under different `PYTHONHASHSEED` values and asserts the same decisions.

### Every fact has one writer

1. **Each fact has one authoritative store, and a copy of a value that changes is derived.** The book requires a single point of truth for any value copied across a system, updated in one place (AoS L8153). DDIA calls the two kinds a system of record and derived data, and where they disagree the system of record is right by definition (DDIA L11985). Here the journal records every intent, order, acknowledgement, fill and cancel when it happens, and the broker is the truth for holdings and cash at the moment the platform asks. The two are independent records that reconciliation compares and never overwrites, and a mismatch halts trading. Positions and cash held anywhere else are derived and rebuilt once the two agree. Captured data, every backup of it or of the journal, and every broker statement the platform saved are recorded facts, not derived copies.
2. **The quantity that sizes an order counts every order already sent, at its worst case for each limit.** The book asks three questions of any replicated read, and one of them decides this case: will the value feed a later write (AoS L7901)? Here the gate and the sizing both measure against the broker's holdings plus every order the journal shows as sent, working or in doubt. For a signed limit, such as net position, the gate checks two cases, every working buy filled with no sell, and every working sell filled with no buy. For gross size, open orders and cash, every working order counts as filled. Choosing filled or unfilled one order at a time can pass two orders that breach a limit together. A strategy's own position comes from the journal's fills and the outside entries assigned to it, and reconciliation checks the strategies' sum against the account. Risk limits, instrument metadata and configuration may be cached with a maximum age. A cache past its age whose refresh fails halts what it governs, a tightened limit applies to the next order, and the refresh, like every session boundary, reads the shared clock rather than the wall clock, so a backtest refreshes the same way live trading does.
3. **One holder of the account's lease sends at a time, and an order with no answer blocks the next.** State that must survive a restart is rebuilt from the journal and the broker, and a rebuild that cannot read either leaves the platform halted. The book's order of preference is to avoid state, then to move it out of the process (AoS L8393, L8827). The lease's mechanism is the one the owner ruled for on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10): an object in S3, renewed by a conditional write, held only around the actions that change the account, and taken over by another instance once it expires unrenewed. Each send waits until durable storage has acknowledged its journal entry, since durability means waiting for the write before acting on it, and DDIA warns that even `fsync` is not guaranteed to work (DDIA L\~6894, L\~6930). For the journal's store that acknowledgement is a successful conditional write. For a local file it is `fsync`, `F_FULLFSYNC` on macOS, and a flush of the directory when the file is created. A write that fails halts trading, and its order is not sent. Each journal entry carries a client order id fixed by the decision, the override entry, the contract-suite run or the close entry before the send, and the broker receives the same id. An order the broker never acknowledged is in doubt rather than failed. It blocks every further order on its account and instrument, and settles only when the broker reports it, when a broker-side expiry the adapter set on it has passed, or when the owner rules on it. A lookup that finds no record never settles it, because a request can still land after its sender has gone (DDIA L4204, L4207). If it stays in doubt past a set time, trading halts and the owner is alerted. A rejection that says the broker already holds the id means the broker has the order, so the order is looked up, never marked failed. [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) does not rely on the broker rejecting a repeated id, so no order is resent. The simulated broker can withhold an acknowledgement, so replay runs this path. These rules go beyond the book, which discards a timed-out call (AoS L8698). When the journal cannot be read, a written procedure for orders in doubt applies, and a test asserts each step.
   1. Halt, and record that the journal is unreadable as the reason.
   2. Cancel working orders through the kill switch, once the journal takes writes again, since each cancel is journaled first. For a store found missing, empty or behind, this step comes after step 3.
   3. Restore from the journal's second copy, then from the latest backup up to its journal position.
   4. Settle the remaining orders from the broker by client order id.
   5. Keep every strategy switched off until the owner assigns the resolved positions, and resume only after a journal entry records them.
4. **The send to the broker is not fenced, so the design names the window that is left and guards it.** A process can pause at any point, in a garbage-collection pause, a suspended virtual machine, swapping or a blocking write, and wake up acting as if it still held its lease (DDIA L9084, L9094, L9102, L9110). DDIA's answer is a fencing token: each grant carries a number higher than every earlier grant, and the protected resource itself refuses a write carrying a lower number than one it has seen, since a client checking its own lease is not enough (DDIA L9274, L9286). For a resource that does not check tokens it suggests working the token into what the resource stores (DDIA L9292). The broker keeps the lease number inside the client order id but never refuses on it, and the book offers nothing for a resource that keeps a token without checking it. So the check of the lease's tag before every broker call, which [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) requires, narrows the window and does not close it. A holder that pauses after its check and before its request leaves can still send after the lease has passed on. Four guards bound that window.
   1. The journal's store refuses a write that names an older lease, in the same atomic step as the write, and a new holder's first act is a journal entry carrying its own lease number.
   2. A new lease number exceeds every lease number in the journal and in the client order ids the broker reports for the account, from the first commit, so a restore from backup cannot reuse one. DDIA's case of a reused number is a promoted replica whose lagging counter reissued keys another store already held, and showed private data to the wrong users (DDIA L4868).
   3. A new holder never resends an older lease's order. The order stays in doubt under rule 3, counted and blocking its instrument.
   4. Reconciliation flags any broker order whose client order id names an older lease and that was missing from the broker's order list the new holder read when it took the lease, and halts. That comparison needs no clock.

   Each guard has its own test, with the other three intact. In the pause scenario the test asserts that the broker holds exactly one order for the decision, and a separate test asserts that a late order under an older lease halts with that order named as the cause.
5. **Each fill applies once, keyed by the broker's execution id, and every decision that reads shared buying power runs under the account's lease, one at a time.** Only an id carried from end to end can drop a duplicate, because each layer below sees only its own retries (DDIA L16438). Every broker message is journaled as received. The uniqueness constraint sits on applying a fill, keyed by its execution id and the broker's correction identifier, in the same step as the write, because a check for a row that does not yet exist has nothing to lock, and two writers can each pass it (DDIA L7635). A repeated id whose content matches the first is applied once. One whose content differs is journaled beside the first and halts trading. The broker adapter states how its broker marks a correction or bust, by a new execution id that names the old one or by a version on the same id, and a correction is a new entry that names the execution it replaces, as a ledger adds a compensating entry rather than editing the old one (DDIA L\~14416). The derived position applies the latest version of each execution once. A test inserts a duplicate straight into the store, bypassing the application's check, and asserts the store refuses it. Every update to derived state sets a value or is keyed by an id that drops a repeat, since an increment applied twice counts twice (DDIA L\~14993). Two strategies that each read the account's buying power and then order against it can each pass the check and together exceed it, the race DDIA calls write skew (DDIA L7538). Its remedy without a transaction is to send every write that might conflict to one partition and process them in order (DDIA L16535), and it finds that one thread in a loop "can sometimes perform better" than concurrent execution by skipping the cost of locks (DDIA L7725). Here the account's lease serializes those decisions, whichever instance holds it. DDIA's serial execution works only when each step waits on no disk or network (DDIA L\~7743, L7821). This design waits on both inside each step, for the lease, the journal's write and the broker's buying power, so its throughput per account is the written scale limit of one lease cycle per order, measured and alarmed under the first section's rules. The holder re-reads the halt state just before each send.

### Recorded data is append-only, and every view of it is rebuilt

1. **Recorded data is never updated or deleted in place, and a correction is a new record.** Append-only files make crash recovery and concurrency simpler, because a crash cannot leave a value half overwritten (DDIA L2484). The engines DDIA describes are append-only on the way in, yet they compact segments by key, keeping only the latest value for each key and dropping the rest (DDIA L2437). So recorded data never sits in a store that compacts by key, unless the writer assigns every record a key of its own, such as its receive sequence, never one taken from the source's fields. The one deletion is the owner's written procedure in the data-cost rule below. DDIA's event sourcing records each action's intent in neutral terms rather than the side effects it had, so a later feature can derive new effects from old events (DDIA L14319). Here the journal records what a strategy asked for as well as the orders that followed, and a cancel or a correction is a new entry that points at the old one. A test rebuilds positions from the journal and compares them with the running state.
2. **A new read view is built from the same record and runs beside the old one, rather than migrating the record.** Separating the form data is written in from the forms it is read in lets a new view run alongside the existing ones, and the old one is shut down once nothing reads it (DDIA L14461, L14470). This is how a stored format changes under the two-release rule below: derived tables move, and captured data and the journal stay as written.
3. **Recorded formats are readable from any language and carry a version, and nothing recorded or read from outside is loaded with Python's `pickle`.** `pickle` is Python's built-in way to save any object as bytes and load it back. A language's own format like that ties the data to the language, tends to neglect versioning, and lets whoever controls the bytes run code when they are loaded (DDIA L3623, L3626, L3632). So every recorded record names the schema it was written with, a registry keeps every schema ever used, and `pickle` never touches captured data, the journal, or anything that arrives from outside the process. Readers keep reading every schema ever written, under the two-release rule below. Data outlives code, so a record written today is read by code that does not exist yet, and a rollback has older code read newer records (DDIA L\~3874, L\~4104). A release passes a compatibility check against every schema in the registry: a field is added only with a default, an identifier is never reused, and a field is removed only if it had a default (DDIA L\~3973). A step that reads a record and writes one back keeps the fields it does not understand, or it drops them silently (DDIA L\~4071, L4319). Identifiers never pass through a float, since a JSON parser that reads numbers as floats corrupts integers above 2^53 (DDIA L3632). Prices and quantities in recorded formats and on the order path are decimals or integer minor units, which is this doc's own rule for exactness, and research may use floats.
4. **Captured data is stored as received, and parsing it into the platform's model is a derived step that can run again.** DDIA finds that making raw data available quickly is often worth more than settling the ideal model first, and leaves its interpretation to the reader (DDIA L12909, L12920). This doc adds its own reason: a parser bug then costs a rerun rather than a lost day.
5. **A backup copies closed segments, names the journal position it is consistent with, and leaves the machine before the first capture is relied on.** A copy taken while writes continue holds parts from different moments, and restoring it makes the mismatch permanent (DDIA L7227). DDIA's segments are never modified once written (DDIA L2449), and this doc infers that a closed segment gives the backup a consistent point to copy. A new copy of a store is a snapshot tied to an exact log position, then every change after that position (DDIA L4777), so each backup records the journal position it covers. A machine in the cloud can vanish without warning (DDIA L293), so a copy of captured data and the journal exists off the machine from the first session on. A capture segment stays open no longer than a stated number of minutes, which bounds what a lost machine can take, and an alarm watches how far the newest off-machine copy trails the head. A restore drill runs on a schedule the operator sets and after every new recorded schema, restores to a scratch place, never over the live store, and checks record counts, checksums and the lease numbers the restored journal holds.
6. **A batch output is written whole to a new place and switched to in one step, and the old output stays until the new one is checked.** DDIA's example loads new files beside the old ones, switches over atomically and can switch back, since the old files are still there (DDIA L12811, L12822). Here that covers backtest results, feature tables and any derived store the loop reads. A multi-part output becomes visible through one small completion record written last, naming the parts, counts and checksums it covers, the way a single node commits when its commit record reaches the disk (DDIA L10857). A reader treats an output with no completion record as missing, which halts under hard limit 3, never as empty. Inputs are read-only, so a run can repeat without harm (DDIA L12311). A job also writes nothing outside its output and reads reference data only from a snapshot placed beside its inputs, because a query to a live store mid-run makes the job slow and its result depend on when it ran (DDIA L12545). Regenerable then means the same records on a second run, which a test checks by building each derived artifact twice in separate processes and comparing a checksum over its sorted records, since a file format may stamp its own creation time (DDIA L13148).
7. **Derived state changes only by consuming the journal in order, and stores the journal position it reflects.** Two writers updating two stores can land in opposite orders with no error, leaving the stores different for good, so no component writes to both the journal and a view of it (DDIA L\~14160). Once an entry is in the journal it is a fact, and no component downstream may reject it. A check runs before the append, or its verdict is a later entry of its own (DDIA L14372). Each derived store saves the journal position it reflects in the same write as the value, so a restore from a snapshot plus the journal's tail equals a full replay (DDIA L\~15043). A test crashes at every write boundary of the update and asserts that the saved position equals the last journal entry reflected in the stored value.

### Coordinate only where a mistake cannot be undone

1. **The order path coordinates, and everything else is asynchronous and may lag.** DDIA separates timeliness, meaning the reader sees the latest state, from integrity, meaning no state is ever wrong. A lapse in timeliness heals on its own and a lapse in integrity stays (DDIA L16612, L16626). Many businesses accept a briefly broken constraint and apologize for it, and paying for coordination only where it is needed buys speed and availability everywhere else (DDIA L16676, L16700, L16711). An order cannot be taken back, so the risk gate and the sizing coordinate through the one sender, while monitoring, reports and research may lag. A lagging view is late and still right only while delivery is reliable, ordered and free of duplicates. A view that misses one message is wrong for good (DDIA L\~16028), which the gap checks above catch. DDIA also leaves the size of a tolerated breach to the business, and notes that coordination trades fewer apologies for inconsistency against more for outages (DDIA L16676, L16693). Here a halt is the outage, and its price is missed trading. This is the reasoning behind the isolation rules above, seen from the data's side.
2. **No transaction spans the journal and the broker, and no consensus algorithm is written here.** Two-phase commit needs every participant to answer, so it spreads one part's failure to all of them, and its coordinator becomes a database whose loss leaves locks held until someone intervenes (DDIA L11110, L11150, L11174). A broker could not take part in one anyway. The journal before the send, a client order id carried end to end and reconciliation reach the same result without one, which is the log-based approach DDIA describes for a transfer between two accounts (DDIA L16554, L16636). The journal, the id and reconciliation reach that result only where the broker rejects a repeated client order id, which the one-writer rules above require the broker adapter to show. DDIA lists the problems that all reduce to agreement among nodes, among them a lock or lease, a uniqueness constraint, a counter with no gaps and an atomic commit (DDIA L\~11482). Here those are the sending lease, the journal's sequence, client order id uniqueness and the block on unknown orders. On [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) they all live in one store, S3, whose conditional writes are an existing compare-and-set, so none of them is consensus written here and none splits the journal's write across two systems (DDIA L11247, L11519). DDIA names three ways a single decider can fail over: wait for it, fail over by hand, or fail over automatically (DDIA L11519). The account's lease fails over automatically, as the owner ruled on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10). The owner is a single decider too, and when the owner cannot be reached the platform stays halted or on paper.

### The code holds its own rules, and every check runs in production

1. **A rule that guards money is held by the code's structure, a type or a test, never by a comment or a convention alone.** McConnell separates programming in a language, limited to what it offers, from programming into one, deciding what the program needs and making the language supply it (CC p. 68, p. 69). Python enforces no boundary between modules, so the import rule test that [#22](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/22) specifies enforces the boundaries, and that issue holds the full rule. In short, every package imports `core` and none of its siblings, only the composition package, `runtime`, imports the broker adapters, and the order path reaches the broker through the `Broker` interface in `core`. An import check guards the wiring and not the call, since a module handed the adapter can call it without importing anything. McConnell's way to make one step's dependence on another visible is to pass the earlier step's output to the later one (CC p. 349). Python lets any module build any type, so the argument shows a reader the order of the steps and a check in the send is what holds it. The send of an order takes the journal's acknowledgement alone, reads the order from the entry it names, and refuses an entry that is missing, not yet durable, or already marked as sending, which covers a forged, swapped or reused acknowledgement. The send also checks the lease's tag and reads the halt state itself, just before the call. A cancel takes an acknowledgement the same way, so it too is journaled before it is sent, including the kill switch's cancels while the journal's store cannot take a write. The owner ruled so on 2026-11-03 on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10), accepting that an S3 outage holds cancels back as a low risk given S3's availability. Until the store takes the write again, the kill switch keeps retrying and the owner is paged.
2. **Every check that guards an order or a record runs the same way in tests and in production.** McConnell's default compiles assertions out of the production build (CC p. 190), though he keeps the code that checks for important errors and the code that lets a program fail safely, which the Mars Pathfinder's engineers left in and used to fix it after landing (CC p. 209, p. 210). Python drops every `assert`, and every `if __debug__:` block, when it runs with `-O`. Here that would make what runs live differ from what was tested, so a guard on the order path or on recorded data raises its exception explicitly. A lint rule refuses `assert` outside the tests, a check refuses `__debug__` outside them, and CI runs the suite a second time under `python -O`. A check that can halt trading, such as reconciliation, runs on the same schedule in every environment, read from the shared clock. Only a check off the order path, such as the scheduled read-back, takes an interval per deployment, and that interval has a maximum written beside the code, so no setting turns a check off.
3. **An absent, unknown or invalid value fails, and no code turns it into a neutral one.** McConnell lists ways to handle a bad value, among them returning a neutral value, substituting the closest legal one, and logging a warning and carrying on (CC p. 194, p. 195). He calls the choice an architectural decision, and defines correctness as returning no result rather than a wrong one, which safety-critical software favors (CC p. 197). Hard limit 3 makes that choice wherever trading reads an input: market data as the loop parses it, the broker's holdings and open orders, the journal at restart, the gate's limits, the clock's readings and the halt state. Capture sits outside it. Capture stores a bad value as received and flags it, and the check runs where the value is parsed, under the faults rules above. The choice takes four forms in the code.
   1. One variable holds one meaning, and no value such as −1 or zero stands for "missing", which McConnell calls hybrid coupling (CC p. 257).
   2. A branch over a closed set, such as the states of an order, names every case and ends in a failure, rather than treating the last case as the default (CC p. 360, p. 363). This is the faults rule's halt on an impossible case (DDIA L9488), written into each branch.
   3. A function's result starts as the failing one, as McConnell's worked example starts its status at "fail" (CC p. 224).
   4. A number is compared with zero explicitly (CC p. 441), and no condition tests a value for truth where None, zero and empty mean different things. The kill switch's loop ends only on a successful broker response that lists no open orders, never on a read that failed.

   A bad value stops new orders and never stops a cancel. The kill switch and each halt keep cancelling the other orders they list, cancel an order whose state is unknown rather than skip it, retry one the broker refused with a definite answer, such as a rate-limit refusal, handle one whose answer was lost under rule 6 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs), and page the owner. A test feeds each input an absent, a stale, an empty, a malformed, an out-of-range, an oversized and a wrong-typed value. It asserts that the halt names the input and the defect, that the alert fired, and that no order reached the broker's stand-in. A second test breaks one broker response in the middle of a kill switch, and asserts that every other order on its list is cancelled.
4. **Data from outside becomes the platform's own types at the adapter, and the raw record is the only thing past it in the vendor's form.** McConnell's barricade checks and converts data at the boundary, so the code inside can treat a bad value as a bug rather than as input (CC p. 203, p. 204). The adapter records what it received unchanged before it converts anything, as the capture and journal rules above require, and only the parser and reconciliation read that raw record. A message that fails conversion stays recorded and halts its consumer. The range and size checks of the faults rules above run at the conversion. A price, a quantity, a currency, a timestamp and an id are distinct types, which is McConnell's case for creating types of one's own (CC p. 311, p. 312), and an id keeps the vendor's value exactly inside its type. A routine that takes two values of one type takes them by keyword (CC p. 180). Each adapter checks the result of every call it makes (CC p. 197). It raises the platform's own exceptions at the level of the interface it presents (CC p. 199, p. 200), keeping rejected, provably not sent, and outcome unknown apart, and counting any library exception it cannot classify as unknown, which leaves the order in doubt under the one-writer rules above. Its exceptions and log lines carry no credential, token or account number, since attackers read error messages (CC p. 196).
5. **A stand-in adapter passes the same contract tests as the real one.** McConnell's statement of the Liskov principle is that every routine of a base class means the same thing in each class derived from it (CC p. 144, p. 145). The simulated broker is the stand-in replay trusts, so one suite of contract tests runs against it, against the paper adapter's own code fed recorded broker responses in CI, and against the broker's paper account on a schedule. The suite covers the cases the one-writer rules depend on: a repeated client order id, a cancel that races a fill, a lookup of an unknown id, a broker-side expiry, a partial fill, a reject, a timeout on an order and on a cancel, a cancel of an order already cancelled, filled or expired, a cancel resent while the first is still pending, a rate-limit refusal on an order and on a cancel, a correction or bust, the side of a buy and of a sell, including a sell that opens a short, and a split that restates a holding, a forced close, a buy-in and an assignment, each where paper can provoke it and a named gap where it cannot. A case paper cannot provoke is named as a gap in the model, and a recording older than a stated age fails the suite. A recording strips credentials, authorization headers, keys in query strings and account numbers before it is written, and a test fails on any committed recording that matches a credential pattern or the account number's format. A stand-in returns an empty answer only where the real system would answer "none", such as a quiet instrument or no open orders, and raises when a read fails or no data exists, so "nothing happened" and "missing" stay two answers.
6. **Positions, P&L, fills and fees are rebuilt in tests by a second, plain implementation, and the comparison is exact.** McConnell's example is an insurer whose rate system was checked by a separate tool that computed each rate on its own, because accuracy was essential (CC p. 722). The second implementation lives under `tests/`, which no module under `src/` imports, so it is a test oracle rather than a second code path. It reads the raw broker messages, drops a repeat by execution id in its own code, and shares no helper with the code it checks. The rebuild test in [Recorded data is append-only](#recorded-data-is-append-only-and-every-view-of-it-is-rebuilt) uses it, and the test asserts exact decimal equality over a count of fills that is not zero. Reconciliation against the broker stays the check that runs on paper and live.
7. **No change made for speed merges without a measurement before and after on recorded data, and a change that gains nothing is reverted.** McConnell's tuning procedure keeps the working version, measures each change alone, and reverts one that does not help, since more than half of attempted tunings gain little or slow the code (CC p. 606). The effect of one optimization depends on the language, its version and its libraries, so experience predicts it poorly (CC p. 603, p. 644). The measurement goes in the pull request, in the percentiles the monitoring rules require.

### Every change can be undone or switched off

1. **A strategy can be switched off without a deploy.** The book's rule is to design anything released so it can be turned off, which is faster than a rollback and leaves the rest of the release running (AoS L4800, L6358, L6996). Here the switch is a per-strategy halt that leaves the other strategies trading. Like every halt under hard limit 3, it stops new orders and never reads as a target of zero, apart from the close after a broker's trade under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control), which only an outside entry for a broker's trade can start. It cancels the strategy's working and in-doubt orders that add to a position, journaled first, and keeps those that reduce one, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [#43](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/43). Switching a strategy off is anyone's to do, while switching it back on in live trading is the owner's, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-02.
2. **A change to a stored format ships in two releases: the code first, the data later.** Old fields and tables stay for as long as a rollback may need them (AoS L6327, L6335). The book puts the price of rollback at under 1% of a release for most changes, and finds the large releases that skip it are the ones that need it most (AoS L6340, L6348). Captured data and the journal are never migrated. A new format applies to new writes, every reader keeps reading every format ever written, and a test replays a journal holding each of them. Before a release, a test replays a journal and capture files the new artifact wrote through the kept artifact's readers, and the release does not go ahead when it fails.
3. **A release is compared with the one before it, and a sudden gain is a finding too.** A jump in throughput can mean a code path or a filter was dropped (AoS L5876). Replays of the same recorded day must match on their counters, such as orders, rejects and fills. Each timing carries a written tolerance in both directions, set from this platform's own run-to-run spread, and the book's worked example uses 5% (AoS L11260). Any change past those bounds without an intended cause goes to the builder (AoS L11226, L11254).
4. **One artifact is built once and is what every environment runs, and configuration may differ by environment only in a committed list of keys.** Newman builds an artifact once so that what is deployed is the thing that was tested (BM L\~2160), and keeps configuration that changes between environments to an absolute minimum (BM L2393). Here the list holds adapter selection and adapter parameters, such as a backtest's date range, a broker's rate limit and the map of partitions to machines, each environment's queue, lease object, and journal and store locations, and references to where each environment's credential is read from, never the credential itself, and the grants the owner's rulings make differ. Each environment's queue, lease and stores sit in that environment's own AWS account, under the owner's 2026-11-02 ruling on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10). The risk gate's hard caps sit outside the artifact and the tracked configuration, in execution's own configuration, which only the owner changes, by hand, and the constraints file can only tighten them, under the owner's 2026-10-29 ruling on [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7#issuecomment-6505752788). A registered strategy's parameters and every code path are identical in backtest, shadow, paper and live, while a research sweep's parameters are its run's inputs, carried with the run's record. A test loads every environment's tracked configuration, fails on a difference outside the list, asserts that no two environments share a queue, a lease or a store, asserts that no tracked file holds a cap, and asserts that a constraints value looser than its cap leaves the cap binding, where how the gate combines the two is [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15)'s proposal for [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7). A key outside the committed list halts new orders, names the key where the halt-state monitor reads it and waits for the owner, and a cap that is missing or unreadable halts new orders, names the cap and waits for the owner, while the release still serves cancels and the kill switch under rule 5. The operator owns the artifact's record. The artifact is the image digest a release pins, whose mechanics [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) holds and [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) proposes, and a worker whose digest differs from the session's halts with "release changed", names itself and waits for the owner, as [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) holds. A mismatched execution worker serves nothing and pages the owner naming the broker's own interface, as a failed startup check of the release's own does, and any other mismatched worker halts new orders only. In slice 1 capture is marketlake's, under the owner's 2026-10-29 ruling on [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5#issuecomment-6502285590), and marketlake's own release keeps it recording, so its hash is read from marketlake's record, never from the trading release's. A capture this platform later runs itself gets its own rule for releasing without a gap in recording. The previous artifact is kept by its digest so a rollback can run.
5. **A release changes only between sessions, and cancels and the kill switch are served while a session is open.** Newman's blue/green deployment runs two versions side by side and switches traffic between them (BM L3044). The account's lease under [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) would serialize two releases' sends, but two versions acting by turns would apply different gate rules, limits or journal formats to one account's orders, and replay could not say which version decided what. So a release changes only at a session's start, under the owner's 2026-11-01 ruling that [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) carries, and a run never mixes releases. [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) holds the mechanics, with [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) as the proposal. Three guarantees hold whatever those mechanics settle. No order from a new release leaves while the old release runs. While a session is open, a release serves cancels and the kill switch. The alarm when none does is monitoring's deadline on each kill switch event and the pull command's own check for execution's entry saying the cancels are done, watched from outside every release, as [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) proposes for [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11), and it reports under the first monitors' heartbeat. Between sessions a pulled kill switch, a fired loss stop, a halt on an unsettled cancel or any other halt that waits for a person persists, the next session reads it before any order, and a switch pulled with no session open alarms the same way. Every order the platform sends expires at the broker by its session's close, as slice 1's market-on-close orders do, and an order type that rests longer waits for a rule on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10). A release serves cancels and the kill switch once its own digest and journal checks pass, because every cancel is journaled first, and a failure of any other startup check, such as a configuration key or the clock, halts new orders only, while a store finding follows rule 3 of [A release is checked where it runs](#a-release-is-checked-where-it-runs-and-nothing-heals-by-hiding-a-fault). A release whose own check fails serves nothing and pages the owner, naming the broker's own interface as the owner's step. An order or a cancel in doubt carries over to the next session under rule 6 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs). An older artifact that cannot read the newer release's journal entries meets the procedure for an unreadable journal, which rule 2 above exists to avoid. The off-machine dead-man check fires when no release sends a heartbeat during a session. A test starts a session with the kill switch pulled, with a loss stop fired and no close open or owed, and with a halt on an unsettled cancel, and asserts no order leaves, fails each startup check other than the release's own and the store checks and asserts the kill switch is still served, fails the release's own digest and then its journal check and asserts a pulled kill switch pages the owner naming the broker's interface, and puts a kill switch event on the queue, leaves it unread past its bound and asserts the alarm fires.

### A release is checked where it runs, and nothing heals by hiding a fault

1. **The checks that clear a release to trade are the code its tests ran, run where the release runs with only the target changed, and the release trades only after they pass.** Farcic runs the same integration tests against the new release twice, with only the address changed: once at its own address before it is made publicly available, and once through the entry point users reach after the switch (DT p. 173, p. 247, p. 248). Here that suite is the charter's Startup step. Its functions run in CI against the simulated broker and replay, and in each environment against that environment's adapters, so the code that clears a release is code the tests ran. No second suite exists beside it, because a second list of checks drifts from the first. A release enables strategies only after Startup passes, a test fails each Startup check in turn and asserts that no order reached the broker's stand-in, a test runs Startup with every check passing and asserts it received no order and no cancel, and a test lets a readiness wait expire and asserts the check fails. A failure follows rule 5 of [Every change can be undone or switched off](#every-change-can-be-undone-or-switched-off), which says what a release still serves after each kind of failed check, and a store finding follows rule 3 below. The book's second run, after the switch, is here reconciliation on every fill under rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) and the synthetic event in shadow under the monitoring rules. No Startup check sends an order or a cancel in any environment, so Startup adds no sender beside the order path and its code is the same everywhere. The synthetic event checks shadow's round trip against the simulated broker, and the first real order is what exercises the real send path, under the operator's check of the first sessions in the charter's Production step. A check that starts something waits on a readiness check with a timeout, never on a fixed sleep, and a wait that expires fails the check. The book calls its own fixed pause something to use rarely (DT p. 200), and this doc makes it never.
2. **The pipeline makes every merge ready to deploy, and deploys none of it.** Farcic's continuous deployment runs from a commit to production with no human intervention (DT p. 20). His continuous delivery stops one step short, where every build that passes could be deployed and a person decides when (DT p. 19). Hard limit 6 makes deploying the owner's, so this platform takes continuous delivery. The pipeline ends at an artifact whose checks passed, recorded under rule 4 of [Every change can be undone or switched off](#every-change-can-be-undone-or-switched-off), and the owner deploys it. No workflow run whose code comes from a branch other than `main` holds any credential, broker or cloud, to a shadow, paper or live account or a data vendor, and no workflow run at all holds a broker credential, since the design on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) gives the broker key to the execution worker's user alone. So the scheduled contract suite against the paper account is not a workflow run. It shares the one paper account under the owner's 2026-11-04 ruling on [PR #29](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/29), so it never runs beside a session or any other test on that account, and its orders meet the hard limits and the account's lease like any other order and never reach a strategy's positions. [#32](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/32) holds how it runs. A deploy accepts only a digest that a run on `main` recorded when it built the commit then at `main`'s head, in a store no other run and no login an agent session holds can write, where that run was started by a push or a manual dispatch, never by `pull_request_target` or `workflow_run`. A credential that deploys the platform is reachable only through a GitHub environment per cloud account, which admits `main` alone and names as its required reviewer an identity no agent session holds, with no administrator bypass, and no login an agent session holds, on GitHub or in the cloud, can change that environment, bypass `main`'s ruleset, assume the owner's role or change who can, or assume a role that deploys or applies the code. Each account's deploy role trusts only its own environment, so approving a paper deploy cannot reach live. A job that uses such an environment runs only on a push to `main` or a manual dispatch on `main`, never on `pull_request_target` or `workflow_run`, which run with `main`'s privileges whatever started them, and it runs nothing but `main`'s head. The build board's Pages environment deploys on its own and is outside this rule, since it holds no credential to the platform. These credential rules are this doc's, not the book's, and [#32](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/32) holds how they are built.
3. **Every machine is built from committed code, a check of that code against what it built finds no difference, and the code cannot destroy what cannot be recovered.** Farcic runs every step from the delivery server, and finds no need to log in to a server and work on it by hand (DT p. 88). A second run of his playbook finds almost nothing left to do (DT p. 190). Here the hosts, their users and grants, the queues and the buckets are declared in committed code, which every environment applies alike, differing only in the keys of the committed list under rule 4 of [Every change can be undone or switched off](#every-change-can-be-undone-or-switched-off), which names where each credential is read from and holds none, whose state stays out of the repository and holds no secret, and whose plan output and deploy logs stay out of any public log or artifact. Applying it to paper or live is a deploy, and so the owner's. An apply that touches execution, its roles and grants, its queue, the host's restart settings and switch, or the stores of the journal, the halt state or the lease happens between sessions, as rule 5 of [Every change can be undone or switched off](#every-change-can-be-undone-or-switched-off) has a release do, since a change to any of them can stop a cancel, unless the owner overrides it for an incident, as [#32](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/32) holds. An infrastructure change rolls back by a new commit on `main`, applied like any other change, that restores the code before it and detaches, never destroys, any protected store the change created. It rolls forward by bringing that store back under the code by its recorded id, as [#32](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/32) builds. The price is that a rollback waits on a merge, which hard limit 6 makes the owner's. The paper gate drills both. After each change and on a schedule, the code runs in a mode that only reports, under a role that cannot write and reads no stored data, and a difference is drift, which alerts. ### Monitoring answers whether there is a problem first

1. **The first monitors answer "is there a problem", and there are between three and ten of them.** Monitoring built bottom-up from host metrics produces alerts that teams learn to ignore, and then a customer reports the outage first (AoS L10210, L10223, L10355). Here the first monitors are these nine.
   1. P&L against expectation.
   2. Exposure against limits.
   3. Fill and reject rates.
   4. Slippage against the cost model, measured as rule 1 of [A fill is measured from the decision](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) says.
   5. Data freshness.
   6. Orders and cancels in an unknown state, and reconciliation mismatches.
   7. Capture-disk headroom, and how far the off-machine copy trails.
   8. Whether the platform or any strategy is halted, and why.
   9. The heartbeat.

   "Where is the problem" and "what is the problem" come after, in that order (AoS L10335).
2. **Every halt is a fixed rule, and statistics only escalate.** The book alerts when a value leaves several standard deviations of its own history at the same time on similar days (AoS L10168, L10313). Here that applies only to counters that behave like rates, such as fills, rejects and message rate, and only after the baseline holds a stated number of sessions. Until then a fixed threshold stands in, and the statistical alert is drilled on recorded history. A limit breach, the loss stop, a reconciliation mismatch and missing or stale input each halt by a fixed rule, because hard limit 3 cannot wait for a statistic. A statistical alert states how often its condition occurs, how often it catches that condition, how often it fires without it and the share of true alarms that follows, or says any of them is unknown. *Fooled by Randomness*, cited below, shows why: a test that misses nothing, with 5% false positives, on a condition that strikes 1 in 1,000 is right close to 2% of the times it fires (FR L\~3304).
3. **Latency is recorded as histograms and reported as percentiles, and histograms combine by adding.** A mean hides how many requests saw a delay, the slow tail is where the busiest accounts tend to sit, and averaging percentiles from several sources is meaningless (DDIA L518, L523, L536, L583). A request that waits on several calls is as slow as the slowest of them, so the tail grows with every call added (DDIA L569). So every latency monitor and every timing in the release comparison is a percentile, such as the median and the 99th, read from merged histograms. A load test sends on its schedule whether or not the last reply has come back, since a harness that waits for each reply keeps its queues artificially short (DDIA L551). Order latency is timed at the broker adapter, where the waiting the caller sees is.
4. **Every queue and buffer states its bound, in time as well as size, and what it does when full, and capture never discards data to stay inside its bound.** A backlog can grow unseen until the disk fills, as when compaction falls behind the writes with nothing to slow them (DDIA L2766). A full queue can drop, buffer or push back on its sender (DDIA L13767). A vendor feed cannot be pushed back on, so capture buffers and spills to disk and never drops a message. Only a queue that feeds a derived consumer, such as the decision loop's latest price, may drop a value a later one replaces, and diagnostic log lines may drop under rule 5 of [A release is checked where it runs](#a-release-is-checked-where-it-runs-and-nothing-heals-by-hiding-a-fault). Each counts every drop. A log kept in a fixed-size ring drops its oldest entries when it fills (DDIA L14037), which for captured data is loss. How far a consumer trails the head of a log is the number to alarm on (DDIA L14056). Here that lag is measured on the capture buffer and on the copy to the off-machine store, and reports under the first monitors' data freshness and headroom, so the list stays at nine. The capture disk alarms at 75% of its limit under rules 2 and 4 of the first section. A journal write that fails halts trading under the one-writer rules.
5. **One id runs from a decision through everything it leads to, and each client order id contains it.** Newman finds that correlation ids are very hard to add later and advises adding them from the start (BM L3354). Here the decision's id is fixed with the decision, from nothing a failover or a restore could wind back, and the risk verdict, the journal entries, the broker calls, every log line and every fill carry it. Each client order id contains that id, or for an order a person placed through the gate its override entry's id, or for a contract-suite order its run's id, or for a close after a broker's trade its close entry's id, each fixed the same way. A decision or a close entry that sends several orders gives each a unique id that still traces back to it, and the rest of the id's format, including where the lease number sits, is [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10)'s to fix. A cancel carries the id of the order it cancels, and the kill switch's own entries carry an id of their own. A test replays a day and asserts that every order, verdict and fill maps to exactly one decision, or for an order a person placed through the gate to its override entry, or for a contract-suite order to its run, or for a close after a broker's trade to its close entry, that every cancel maps to the order it cancels and names what sent it, a decision, an override, a halt, the kill switch or a contract-suite run, or the cancel a repeat repeats, that outside, close, lease and halt entries carry a kind of their own, and that a decision sending two orders gets two client order ids that share its id.
6. **A synthetic event runs through the whole loop on a written schedule, in replay or in a shadow deployment against the simulated broker, and nothing built from it reaches the paper or live account.** Newman's example is an investment bank's trading system, where a fake event entered every minute or so and an alert fired when its result did not appear, which he found a far better sign of trouble than lower-level metrics (BM L3250, BM L3300). Here a shadow deployment of the same artifact runs beside paper and live for as long as they run, and the event enters only that loop's own input files, never captured data and never a file the paper or live loop reads. A marker carries from the event to every record built from it. In replay and shadow the gate holds a marked decision to every limit and routes it to the simulated broker alone. In paper and live the gate refuses a decision built on a marked input, and the broker adapters refuse any marked order and journal it as provably not sent, and each refusal halts, alerts as misrouting and waits for the owner, with a test for each. The comparison of shadow's orders with a replay of the same day leaves marked records out, and a drill-halted session's records after the halt, as [A fill is measured from the decision](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) says. The event watches shadow's loop and reports as shadow's, so a fault confined to paper's or live's broker connection, lease or account is left to the heartbeat and the other first monitors. Replay and shadow run as a user that cannot read the broker's credential. The result reports under the first monitors' heartbeat, so the list stays at nine, and a result missing past a written bound alerts the owner. The operator owns it, and the paper gate's alarm drills include it.

### A strategy is judged over a sample, and runs unchanged through it

Mark Douglas's book *Trading in the Zone* (Prentice Hall Press, 2000) supplies the rules for judging a strategy and for the human acts around it. A citation like TZ L2038 is the Kindle location of one of the owner's highlights. One like TZ L\~2776 points into the book's full text at a location interpolated between the highlights, good to a few locations. The book writes for traders who decide each trade themselves, and its remedy is a change in how the trader thinks. Here the strategies are code, so the design holds by construction most of what Douglas asks a trader to learn, and these rules keep it so. What follows "Here" in a rule is this project's rather than Douglas's. What still binds people is the owner's own acts, and the agents that build, judge and change strategies. The book gives no data for its figures, such as its claim that fear causes 95 percent of a trader's errors (TZ L1068), so none of them is adopted. Six rules follow.

1. **Every entry, exit and size comes from the strategy's declared inputs, and each position's worst loss is known before an order adds to it.** Douglas defines an edge as better odds for one outcome over the others (TZ L\~2209), and defines it so precisely that no judgement in the moment decides whether it is present. He gives a losing trade an exit that says how much must be risked to learn whether it works (TZ L3092, L3095). He holds that the traders who win consistently are the ones who set their risk before they trade, and counts failing to predefine risk, cut losses and take profits by a system among the commonest and costliest errors (TZ L506, L1661, L1663). Here a strategy's specification names its exit rule, which need not be a price level. The slice-1 spread exits when its z-score crosses zero, so its loss at the exit cannot be known in advance, and its worst loss comes from the stress move the hard caps hold, as the vocabulary defines **worst loss**. The project adds the reason: a gap, a halted feed, or a halt that stops the exit order can carry the price past any exit. An order counts as reducing only when it reduces both the strategy's position, or a contract-suite run's, and the account's under every case of which working and in-doubt orders fill, and the part of it that crosses zero counts as adding. A stress move that is anything but a finite positive number inside its sane range, from a vintage within its written age, is invalid input, so it leaves the worst loss uncomputable, and it halts new orders under hard limit 3 and waits for the owner, as a missing cap does, cancelling nothing and keeping every working order. The risk gate refuses an order that adds to a position whose worst loss it cannot compute, journals the refusal with the term it lacked and alerts the owner. It never refuses an order that only reduces the position for want of that figure, though a halt in force still holds that order, apart from the stops case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) names for the close after a broker's trade. A contract-suite order belongs to no strategy's position. It passes this check like any other order: its run names the position it adds to, and the gate refuses it when that worst loss cannot be computed. [#32](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/32) is to name how. The gate refuses and never resizes. Sizing makes the whole target smaller, every leg together, and never moves an exit to fit. Douglas tells a trader whose total risk feels too large to trade a smaller size and leave each trade's risk where the research put it (TZ L3220). [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) holds the mechanism and its tests, for [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) and [#9](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/9) to take up.
2. **A strategy is judged over a sample registered before it starts, never by one trade or one streak.** Douglas holds that wins and losses fall at random within any set of variables that defines an edge, so a single outcome says nothing about the edge (TZ L2038). He judges success over a sample of trades, large enough to test the edge fairly and small enough that a fading edge shows before it has lost too much (TZ L3186, L3198). The owner ruled on 2026-11-02 on [#4](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/4) that a strategy's registered test runs on paper, and that backtest and shadow run no registered experiment. So a strategy's registration names seven things before its sample opens on Paper and, as this project's own rule, again before Live. Most of the seven are this project's own rather than the book's.
   1. The size of the sample, in trades or in sessions, with an upper and a lower bound on trades, and its longest span in sessions, pauses included. A sample ends at the first of its registered size, its upper bound on trades and its longest span, and one that ends below its lower bound ends without a verdict.
   2. The measures the sample is judged by, net of costs, with the holding terms rule 4 of [A fill is measured from the decision](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) asks a sample to name. Every measure, and the retiring check, counts every position the sample opened, and one still open at the end is marked at recorded prices, net of the cost to close it.
   3. The result that counts as the edge holding, with the smallest effect worth acting on, which the sample must be able to detect at its lower bound on trades, and the level and power behind it.
   4. The range of drawdowns and losing streaks to expect, taken from the in-sample backtest and widened by a rule registered with it, so the range draws on no data held out of the backtest.
   5. The result that retires it, and when that result is checked.
   6. The most human overrides the sample takes before it ends, and the longest total time that pauses a person starts or ends may take across the sample, past which rule 3 says how the decisions they cover are scored.
   7. The largest share of the decisions that asked a change the sample may score by replay rather than by its stage's own fills, counting every such decision a pause skipped. The owner's ruling on [#4](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/4) has the test read paper's own fills, and this share keeps a passing result resting on them. Past that share the sample ends without a verdict.

   The passing result is judged across every sample the project has opened toward promotion, across strategies and versions and ended early or not, under the false-discovery-rate control that CLAUDE.md's [research pins](../CLAUDE.md#research-pins) require, which the first registration declares, and this project widens that batch past the pin's one search, so a sample cannot be restarted until one happens to look good. The book's own sample of at least 20 trades comes from the author's experience rather than from data (TZ L3198), so it is not adopted. A losing streak longer than any the backtest saw is to be expected in time, so the range guides the people reading results, and the loss stop and the caps never rest on it. Douglas judges at the end of a completed sample. Checking the retiring result earlier is this project's own rule, since a halt opens no new position. The check is a fixed rule beside the loss stop, never a report that may fail. Reaching the result switches the strategy off under the per-strategy halt in [Every change can be undone or switched off](#every-change-can-be-undone-or-switched-off), with "retired" as its reason, pages the owner, and ends the sample. That switch-off does not hold the close under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control). Requiring a new registration to release that halt is this project's own rule, and in live trading switching the strategy back on stays the owner's. Numbers from a sample that ended without a verdict, and decisions reported apart, are labelled exploratory. Every end of a sample alerts the owner, and the retiring check runs for as long as the strategy trades or holds a position. A strategy whose retiring check or outcome match has not completed within its written time halts on a fixed rule and pages the owner, and a stale check of orders back to their causes halts all trading the same way. Once the check completes the halt releases under rule 4 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs), at most a written number of times a session, ending only itself, and in live trading it waits for the owner. The dead-man check remains for when the platform cannot halt itself.

   A strategy may run on Paper with no sample open, including past the end of a sample, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [#4](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/4), because the walking skeleton's paper run tests the platform rather than the strategy. That run's numbers are labelled exploratory, and none of its decisions counts toward a sample, even one registered later. Leaving Paper still takes a registered sample that runs to its end and passes, and the step to Live still takes a registration. A run no registration has reached has no retiring result, so only the caps and the loss stop bound it. Past a sample's end on Paper the retiring check its registration named stays on. A registration absent or unreadable once the journal shows its sample opened, or on a live account, is missing input under hard limit 3. Every halt and page this section adds has fired in a drill before a sample opens on Paper, from input the drill injects under its own journal entry, so the drill spends no registration. The price is that those sessions count toward no promotion, so a strategy's registered evidence starts later. [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) holds the registration and its report.
3. **The strategy runs unchanged through its sample, every change it asks for is accounted for, and a change to its decision path closes its sample.** Douglas's exercise trades the system exactly as designed for every occurrence of the edge in the sample, with no outside input (TZ L3232), because taking every edge is what lets the sample show the edge's odds (TZ L1860, L3235). He asks for rules that are rigid and expectations that are flexible (TZ L2026). Here the strategy's **decision path** stays fixed through a sample, by version. A change to it closes the sample without a verdict and counts as one more variant under the research pins. So does a raise of the strategy's size or of a limit it trades under. A stress move lowered below its value at the sample's opening raises the net cap, so it closes the sample even when its calculation was named before the sample opened, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [PR #31](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/31). A cut to its size, a tightened limit or its return to the value it held when the sample opened, and a limit that moves under a rule set in advance, such as the net cap following the account's value, are inputs that keep the sample open, and the report judges results per unit of risk. In live trading only the owner returns a tightened limit to that value, since the return raises risk, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [PR #31](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/31). A platform change outside the decision path keeps the sample open once a replay of the sample's recorded sessions shows the strategy's orders unchanged, which speaks only for the sessions replayed and for what the simulated broker can show. A difference counts as a change to the decision path. A change to what rule 2 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) commits closes the sample all the same, on purpose, though it touches no order. A fix to the gate or a halt, and a rollback, are never held back to keep a sample whole. Every change the strategy asks of its target ends in one recorded outcome per order, or one for the request where no order followed. Every order traces to a request, a human override, a contract-suite run or a close entry, and every cancel to a request, a human override, a halt, the kill switch or a contract-suite run, and a repeat of a lost cancel to the cancel it repeats. A contract-suite run counts only on the paper account, and only outside a session, until the owner rules how one may run on a live account. A close order counts as traced only when its close entry passes the check in part 2 of case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control). An outside entry counts as traced only as rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) allows, which for an order or a cancel takes the broker's own record of a trade it made or the owner's confirmation. The running platform checks both directions at a set interval, and shows under the halt-state monitor how many it checked. A request counts as unmatched only once a written time has passed since the strategy published it, shorter than the time to the strategy's next decision. Until then it is in flight, and an order in doubt stays pending under the clock of rule 3 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer). A request whose outcomes do not match halts its strategy, pages the owner and waits for the owner, or on shadow and paper the operator. An order or cancel that traces to nothing halts all trading, apart from the close case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) lets through for the orders a confirmation names, pages the owner and waits for the owner, since it is the mark of a second sender or a path around the gate. A skip comes from a closed list in the strategy's specification and never answers an absent, stale or invalid input, which halts under hard limit 3. A request lost without a record changes the sample with nobody deciding to, which is the same failure as reading no signals as hold nothing. A halt the rules set in advance pauses the sample: the loss stop, a halt the platform raises, and a drill journaled as one before it starts. A stop of the platform itself pauses the sample from its last journaled decision, and startup journals that pause with its cause. A person's kill switch is no pause, and rule 4 scores it. A pause stops new decisions counting toward the sample's size, while every position held through it keeps counting its results, and each decision the strategy would have made during it is scored by replay where prices exist and reported apart. A pause that a person starts or ends, such as a drill, a loss stop a person releases, or a restart that waits for a person, can still choose what the sample sees, so past its registered total the decisions it covers are scored as rule 4 scores a stop, counting toward the registered share and never toward the most overrides. The hard limits win over the book's "no matter what". Each halt on the retiring check, a stale check, a mismatched request or an untraced order or cancel cancels working and in-doubt orders that add to a position, journaled first and handled as rule 3 of [The code holds its own rules](#the-code-holds-its-own-rules-and-every-check-runs-in-production) and rule 6 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) say, and keeps those that reduce one. A lost registration's halt, like rule 1's, cancels nothing and keeps every working order. [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) holds the outcome records, the running match and their tests, for [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) and [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11).
4. **A human act that departs from the strategy is journaled with its reason, and the decisions it touched stay in the sample, scored as the strategy's own.** Douglas calls trading without a plan random, because it cannot show what works and what does not (TZ L559). He lists the ways a trader shields himself from a losing position, such as making a case for staying in it (TZ L867). The charter already journals a human override like an order. Overrides can be expected to cluster on decisions that look bad at the time, so dropping the decisions they touched would leave a selected subset. The project's answer is to score each such decision as the strategy's own would have played out, by a replay of the decision path at recorded prices, and keep that score in the sample, with the override's own effect reported apart. A person's switch-off or kill switch counts the same way for every decision while the strategy is off, since capture runs outside every halt and the prices exist, and so does a decision refused because a person tightened a limit. Past the registered most overrides, the sample ends without a verdict and still counts in the batch. A switch-off, the kill switch, a cancel or a cut in size never counts toward that most, and no count on a sample delays or refuses one. Each decision scored by replay still counts toward the registered share, so the price of a long stop is that the sample can end without a verdict, and the project accepts that price. A person's change to a position through an override, or any change at the broker the owner confirms, leaves that strategy switched off until a person switches it back on, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [PR #31](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/31), because execution trades the difference between target and position and would otherwise buy the change back at the next decision. Every decision while it stays off is scored as a person's switch-off is. That switch-off is durable no later than the journal entry recording the change, and no decision runs between them. It sorts orders as any switch-off does with the override's own order counted as working, and it neither cancels nor holds that order. A trade the broker made that execution journals switches its strategy off the same way and for the same reason, and it counts toward no most. Neither does the close after a broker's trade that follows it, whose fills count in that position's result like any other fills. Switching it back on accepts that the next decision trades the difference, and in live trading that switch stays the owner's. Switching a strategy off stays anyone's to do, and it takes effect at once. It is acknowledged to whoever made it only once its journal entry, with who made it and why, is durable. As this project's own rule, after a stop that leaves no instance of the environment running and no clean-shutdown record, every strategy stays off under a halt that waits for a person, with the reason where the halt-state monitor reads it. Its adding orders are cancelled as rule 3 says and its reducing orders kept, through the order path after Startup's checks, since Startup sends nothing, and the platform pages the owner, naming each open position, until the owner confirms, or on shadow and paper the operator does. A lease taken by a running instance is no such stop, and neither is one component's supervised restart. The price is that a lost machine is replaced without a person, while trading resumes only on a confirmation. On shadow and paper a session holding the operator's role counts as a person under this section, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [PR #31](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/31), because no money is at stake there. The price is that paper does not rehearse the human step, so the owner gives this confirmation in one drill before Live. [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) holds the mechanism, for [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) to take up.
5. **No strategy, agent or proposal raises risk after a run of wins, except on a rule or evidence set out before the run.** Douglas holds that a trader is most likely to break rules, overtrade and oversize while winning, and that a larger position magnifies the next move against it (TZ L919, L943). Here sizing and every limit follow rules the owner set in advance, such as the net cap that grows and shrinks with the account's value, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-01 on [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7). No strategy raises its size from its own recent results except through such a rule. A proposal to raise a cap or a share of the account cites evidence named before the run, such as a registered sample's passing result or a recorded vintage. A recorded vintage supports a lower stress move only where the calculation over it was named before the run of wins, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 on [PR #31](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/31). A proposal to take the step to Live cites a registered sample's passing result on the exact version of the decision path it proposes, so a change to that path before the step needs a passing sample of its own, and a retiring result reached after that pass withdraws it. Neither cites a winning streak or elapsed time, and the decision stays the owner's. [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) holds the test.
6. **Before a strategy goes live, the board states what a bad first sample can cost.** Douglas asks a trader to know the risk of every trade in the sample before it starts, and to accept that risk without discomfort (TZ L3207, L1081). He treats losses as a cost of doing business (TZ L2092). Here the board's sitting before the Live step states three things side by side, per unit of the capital cap the owner will set.
   1. The sample's upper bound on trades times each position's worst loss.
   2. The sample's longest span in sessions times the daily loss stop.
   3. That a gap past the stress move breaks both, and that worst loss covers only the stress move's window, so a position held longer can lose more than that figure with no gap and no halt. In slice 1 the only order a halt lets go out to close a position is the close after a broker's trade, so a position any halt leaves open, including one that close did not reach, has no strategy exit, and only the owner's act bounds it.

   A drawdown inside the registered range is not by itself a reason to change the strategy, and anyone may still switch it off at any time.

### A result is read against the luck that could have produced it

Nassim Nicholas Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* (2nd ed., Random House, 2004) supplies the rules for what a result can and cannot show. It was read as the first volume of the Kindle *Incerto* bundle. A citation like FR L1855 is that bundle's Kindle location at one of the owner's highlights. One like FR L\~2558 points into the book's full text at a location interpolated between the highlights, good to a few locations. Its cast, such as John and the dentist, is invented, and most of its figures show arithmetic rather than measurements, so no figure of its own is adopted as a threshold. The book writes for people who trade by hand and read their own results. Here the strategies are code, so what follows "Here" in a rule is this project's rather than Taleb's. Nothing here adds a halt, and [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) holds what the rules ask of each record, for the issues that build those records. Five rules follow.

1. **A number quoted for a strategy states how many tries stand behind it, and a result picked by a search states what the same picking scores with no edge.** In Taleb's invented fair game, 10,000 managers leave 313 with five winning years in a row by luck alone (FR L\~2558), and the best record on show depends more on how many started than on any one manager's odds (FR L\~2575). A backtester that tries enough rules finds one that worked on past data (FR L\~2678). Here a result quoted on any surface, such as a pull request, a report or a registration, states the count of variants, parameter settings and universes tried to reach it, the dropped ones and those tried in a sibling repository included, beside the label [research pins](../CLAUDE.md#research-pins) require. A try nobody noted cannot be counted, so such a result says its count is unknown. A candidate that arrived from a screen or a suggestion counts the whole population it was picked from, because Taleb asks for a stricter standard for an investment that comes to the buyer (FR L2626). A result picked by a search also states what the same selection code's pick scores on a population of the same size with no edge, such as random signals, over a stated number of runs. That test is this project's rather than the book's, and it sits beside the false-discovery-rate control of those pins.
2. **A backtest states how its universe was chosen, and keeps the members that later failed.** Taleb's example is a study whose sample held only managers still in business, so those who did badly and closed never counted, and he would start from the population of five years ago and run forward (FR L2489). A claim about what the winners share needs the same trait's rate among the losers, as his case of a study of millionaires shows (FR L\~4169). Here a universe a rule selects reads only what was knowable on each date through the point-in-time interface, and instruments that later delisted stay in it, in the security master [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) holds. A universe picked by hand, such as slice 1's, is named as hand-picked wherever its result is quoted. A source that lacks dead instruments is named as a gap in the result rather than filled by what survived.
3. **A strategy is judged by what it makes and loses, and a sample states what it could not show.** A bet that pays $1 at odds of 999 in 1,000 and costs $10,000 otherwise wins nearly always and loses about $9 on average (FR L1855), so how often something succeeds does not matter when failure costs too much (FR L558). One large bet shows little about who holds the advantage, while the same money in many small bets shows it (FR L\~3891). Data can refute a claim and never prove it (FR L2136), and the best record can belong to whoever's run happened to be free of the rare event (FR L\~1829). Here a hit rate is never quoted without figures for the size of wins and losses and how concentrated the profit is, and [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) holds which figures a sample's report gives. A passing result reads as evidence of an edge at its registered level over the conditions the sample held, and its report names the stress conditions the sample did not hold. A strategy whose record is many small gains names the rare event that would take them back (FR L\~721). A null result is pinned as not shown, with the sample's size and the smallest effect it could have detected at a stated level and power, and never as no effect, the error Taleb describes in a journalist's report of an inconclusive trial (FR L\~3362).
4. **Every limit on what can be lost states the worst case it assumes, a strategy's own record never sets that case, and rarity is never the cause given for a loss.** Taleb's two invented traders read their bets and their risk off the same past data (FR L2309). With a skewed payoff, the variance a sample shows usually falls below the true one (FR L\~4274). Here the worst case behind a limit, such as the stress move, never takes its value from a strategy's backtest or paper record. [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) holds how each is derived. The registered range of drawdowns and the retiring result judge a strategy against its own expectation, so neither is a limit's worst case. Calling a loss a ten sigma event says more about the model than the market (FR L\~3740), so the review of a loss past the worst case a limit assumed names the assumption that failed, never rarity or bad luck.
5. **A decision is reviewed on what was known when it was made, with the same questions after a win as after a loss, and on a short read only a fixed rule acts on its own.** A mistake is judged by the information available at the time, not after the fact (FR L1286), and Taleb prefers the epic standard, which rates conduct over outcome (FR L3786). People credit their wins to skill and their losses to randomness (FR L3758). So a review of a strategy's result or a human override reads the inputs as they stood by knowable time first, and says whether the registered rules were followed, before any profit or loss. Over a year the dentist's portfolio gains with 93% probability and over a second with about 50%, at the same return (FR L1424), so the shorter the read, the more of it is noise (FR L1437). Here the safety checks keep reading through every session, and what acts on its own is a fixed rule, such as the loss stop, a halt or the retiring check. This rule delays no switch-off, kill switch, cancel, cut in size or rollback, each of which stays under rule 3 or 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it), and raising risk follows rule 5 there. A move inside a strategy's usual spread is not read as evidence about the strategy (FR L3419), and [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) holds how the daily report shows it. A halt, a fault or a rule breach is named whatever the size of the move.

### A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails

Rishi K. Narang's *Inside the Black Box* (3rd ed., Wiley, 2024) draws the boxes of the charter's module table, and this repository first cited it from the owner's highlights alone. These rules come from reading the whole book. A citation like L4331 is a Kindle location of one of the owner's highlights or of a screen of the book's full text. The book writes for investors choosing a quant manager, and its firms are large, so what follows "Here" in a rule is this project's rather than Narang's. It gives no number for a plateau, a broken relationship or a cost margin, and its figures are examples, so none is adopted as a threshold. These rules add no new kind of stop, but rules 4, 5 and 6 add ways to reach the ones that exist, and rule 7 asks a session holding the operator's role to switch off a strategy on shadow and paper when an error can change an order, a position or a recorded value, or keep a guard from stopping one. Under rule 4 an order past a cap that reads no price meets the gate's refusal, which adds no halt. Under rule 5 a snapshot that cannot be stored, named or confirmed, or a fill with nothing behind it, halts new orders. Under rule 6 a split the record and the broker disagree on, a record of splits that a series needs and that cannot be read or falls short of the dates a read spans, and a vintage with no stated price basis each halt, and a record that leaves a large move unexplained meets the halt [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) records. None stops a cancel beyond what a stored halt already does to a plan's own. Rule 2 adds a condition for a sample to open and a way for one to end, and neither adds a halt, though the owner's ruling under it can add a stop on new entries. Rules 1 and 3 add no check at run time. [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks of each issue what these rules need from its build. Seven rules follow.

1. **A backtest has one fill rule by order type, names any fill a live order could not have reached, and when quoted as evidence also shows what it earns acted on late.** Narang's example is a set of analyst downgrades of Microsoft that all came before the open, so the stock had already lost about 11 percent, and a simulation must not assume a trade early enough to catch that move (L4331). Here the point-in-time interface already limits what a decision reads. The simulated broker has one fill rule by order type for every run, with no branch for any test, and a fill uses no price older than the decision's newest input. A fault or a contract case reaches the simulated broker as a value at its edge, so the fill rule holds no branch. [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) holds how each order type fills. A backtest that reads a session's close and fills at that close assumes a price no order sent after that read could reach, since a live run decides before the close, so it names that gap. A replication pin, such as acceptance test 1, reproduces a recorded answer and claims no edge, so its number is never quoted as evidence for the strategy. Many quants also test what a strategy earns when its trades start late (L4270). Narang's S&P 500 example, which he says tends to be early, earned 870 percent in total when delayed one day against 746 percent on time, both before costs, and he doubts that is a good sign (L4331). Quoted as evidence means offered on any surface in favour of a strategy, and a result that kills an idea owes none of the extra runs here or in rule 3. Here a backtest quoted as evidence, on data the strategy's research already read and never on a held-out window or a registered sample's sessions, also shows what its recorded quantities earn acted on one decision late. That figure is computed from the run's recorded quantities and prices, with the on-time run's fill rule, accounting and cost model, so no module holds a late branch, no order rests across a decision, and at a lag of zero it reproduces that run exactly. It forms no order and calls no broker, and it shares the code of all three with the on-time run. The price is that it meets no gate, halt or cutoff, so it shows no refusal a delay would cause, and it charges the cost model on the delayed trades. For a backtest that reads the close, the delayed quantities earn from the next close, where a live order could fill, though that is not the schedule a live run keeps. An improvement on the late figure shows nothing on its own and promotes nothing, because a strategy with no edge can show one too. Where a backtest fills at the close it read, the late figure is the reachable one, so a result that disappears on it can kill the idea, and the price is that a real edge lasting one session disappears the same way.
2. **A strategy that opens a sample states the bet it makes, when that bet loses, and what would show the bet has broken.** Narang asks a quant whether the bets are relative and what relative means (L5552), and expects a strategy's return pattern to fit the account given of it (L5590). A relative strategy assumes a stable link between its instruments, and an unstable link dooms it (L3168). His airline pair held a narrow spread for long periods, and March 2020 could have knocked a trader out (L1255). Schwab and Merrill Lynch went through four phases from 1996 on (L4690), and the S&P 500's correlation with Japan's Nikkei index ran from +0.01 to +0.66 depending on when it was measured (L2905). Among quants who trade groups, most use ones larger than pairs (L1744). The book gives no test that tells a break from a drawdown. Here, before a strategy's sample opens on Paper and again before Live, its specification commits beside the registration what it bets on, why the effect should hold and, where it rests on a link between instruments, why that link should, the conditions under which it expects to lose, the sign of return it expects there, one reading on recorded data that would show a break, and that reading's range. These stay fixed through the sample. A change to what they commit, such as a range, closes it without a verdict, a spelling fix does not, and no sample opens without them. The Live commit restates what the passing sample ran under, and a change to it needs a passing sample of its own. A strategy with no sample open owes none of this. Its range is built without its named break periods, each named by a dated market event, and the report shows the reading's values in each break beside the range and how often it raised a false alarm, on sessions the range was not computed from, all labelled exploratory. The range, its break figures and the daily reading come from one calculation through the point-in-time interface. A held-out window and a registered sample's sessions stay out of all of it, and [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) item 4 holds the rest. [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks of [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) where the report scores the sign, labelled exploratory. Once a sample is open the daily report shows the reading beside its range and names one outside it, as [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks of [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11). It feeds no decision on shadow, or on paper unless the owner's ruling the charter's [Live stage](team-charter.md#5-release) asks for brings a stop, which then runs through the whole sample that ruling comes before. Narang's story of a fund that stayed invested through August 2007 and recovered (L4961) is one case, so it settles nothing about holding through a break.
3. **A backtest quoted as evidence names the cost it assumed, shows a higher one too, and shows the settings beside the one chosen.** Narang's example makes $0.10 per 100 shares at no cost and loses $0.90 once trading costs a cent a share (L4530). Too low a figure trades too much and too high a figure holds too long, and he would rather err high, though getting it about right is better still (L2387, L4530). Narang also prefers a setting that sits among good neighbours to one that scores better alone, since the lone peak is more likely an accident of the sample (L4387). Here every quoted result names its cost model's version. A backtest quoted as evidence, on data the strategy's research already read and never on a held-out window or a registered sample's sessions, also shows a higher cost and the settings on each side of each chosen one, and says whether its sign changed and by how much it moved. The cost's margin and each setting's step are written before the run, with the reason for their size. A backtest on data its research read stays exploratory, and so does each run beside it, and adopting a delay, a cost or a neighbouring setting because of them counts as a new variant. Narang adds, with no data, that a strategy holding for a year needs centuries of returns before anyone can analyse it (L5417). A slow strategy makes few independent bets, so a sample's report states its count of fitted settings beside the count of independent decisions [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) asks for.
4. **A limit states the exposure it bounds and why it sits at that level, and a cap that reads no price backs the data check.** Narang separates the exposure a strategy is paid for from the risks that come with it (L2067), limits those the alpha model does not forecast (L2142), and asks what the limits are and why they were set there (L5552, L5590). Rule 4 of [A result is read against the luck that could have produced it](#a-result-is-read-against-the-luck-that-could-have-produced-it) makes a limit state its worst case. Here each limit also states the exposure it bounds and why it sits at that level rather than half or double. Equal risk weight reads the past, and bank stocks stayed calm for years before 2008 (L2733). Equal weights across many positions also blunt a price wrong by 100 times, which could fool an alpha model into an enormous position (L2696). This project adds that a cap stated in dollars reads the same wrong price as the size it caps, so at least one of the hard caps on each order and each position reads no price, and [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) to set the levels. A test feeds a wrong price past the data check, through the next step's normal interface with no switch in the code under test, and the gate stays on the path. It fails if an order past such a cap reaches the broker's stand-in. That refusal is journaled and alerts the owner. A wrong price can still pass inside a cap, and [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) lists the cases. A reducing order inside the order cap, on a leg that reads a gain, rests on the data check alone, so a wrong price that passes that check can sell up to that cap, and no limit here stops it. The same wrong price can read as a gain on a held position and keep the loss stop silent, and a price read low buys more shares, bounded only by the cap with no price in it. These three gaps stay open on shadow and paper, and the charter's [Live stage](team-charter.md#5-release) says what must happen to each before a sample's pass can be cited for Live. The price is that a cap with no price in it stays put as the account grows, so growth past its headroom takes a raise, which closes an open sample under rule 3 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it), and that an order cap below a position refuses that position's full exit, since the gate refuses and never resizes.
5. **Each fill traces to what calibrating the cost model needs, from the first order, and a fitted cost model states the sizes it was fitted on.** A flat model is close to right only near one trade size (L2554), and a linear one overstates small trades and understates large ones (L2585). Narang's better models evolve from a firm's own recorded trades (L2509), which he calls perhaps the most neglected data it holds (L3768). Here each fill traces, through the id it carries, to the price its decision read, with the source's time and the time the platform received it, and to the session's official bar in a recorded vintage. That price sits in the snapshot the decision read, which counts as captured data wherever this doc says what must be kept or survive, because a value not kept when the decision read it cannot be recaptured. It is written once under its run's key, and a second read, even one that differs only in its received time, is stored beside it and never replaces it. A snapshot the gate read or a halt refused counts the same way. A halt raised here whose write failed or got no answer holds its process as the first of the seven things in item 2 of the opening of [A fill is measured from the decision](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control) says. It goes to the store that holds the journal, acknowledged by durable storage before the journal entry that names it, so it adds no dependency to the order path. A decision's new order goes out only with its snapshot stored and named. A write that fails or gets no answer, an entry whose snapshot the store does not hold or cannot confirm, and a decision's order entry that names none each halt new orders and page the owner, keeping working orders and cancelling only as [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) rules, and wait for the owner, or on shadow and paper the operator. That halt stops a plan's own cancel as any stored halt does, under [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10), and no other cancel. The price is that one unanswered read stops trading until a person clears it. [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) and [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) for how, and [#8](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/8) for the rest. This adds no market price read at the send and no input to any decision. A fill with no decision is an override's or a contract-suite run's, or comes from a close after a broker's trade, and says so, and any other fill with nothing behind it halts under rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs). The price is that the drift between the decision and the send stays unmeasured unless capture covers the send, which on this platform is minutes, and a strategy that trades within a session would need it. Once a cost model is fitted, it names the sizes and instruments it was fitted on.
6. **A split reads as neither a gain nor a loss.** A 3:1 split the vendor never recorded reads as a fall of about two thirds (L3856). Here USO's one-for-eight reverse split took effect on 2020-04-29, by Alpha Vantage's split record read on 2026-11-05, inside the history the stress move reads, so a series read as traded across that date shows a gain of 700 percent. A split record counts as captured data wherever this doc says what must be kept or survive, a correction is a new record, and a recorded vintage stays unchanged. Every reader of a price takes the split from one place, which [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) to name, by its knowable time through the point-in-time interface, so no split applies twice and a series its source already adjusted takes none. A vintage with no stated price basis is missing input. Reconciliation compares each split record taking effect while an instrument is held with the broker's record of it, before any new order on that instrument from the split's effective date, and any difference, a holding not yet restated included, halts under rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs), and that rule says what happens to working orders. An outside entry leaves that halt in place until the two records agree, and that rule says who clears it after that, so on a live account only the owner does. For a series that takes a split from it, an unreadable record of splits, or one that does not reach the dates a read spans, is missing input under hard limit 3, never "no split", and its halt keeps every working order. What a split record leaves unexplained reads as a real move. Where that move raises the spike flag it halts the decision as [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) records, keeping working orders and cancelling only as [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) rules, and waits for the owner, or on shadow and paper the operator. Three gaps stay open on shadow and paper, and the charter's [Live stage](team-charter.md#5-release) says what must happen to each before a sample's pass can be cited for Live. A smaller error passes as a real move, a false record on an instrument not held can hide one, and a wrong ratio in history can hide one from the stress move and so raise the net cap. [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) to write the threshold that bounds the first. No split raises what a cap allows in dollars, and [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) asks [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) how, by a change that can only tighten. [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) item 8 holds the test. A ticker can also pass to another company, as V passed from Vivendi to Visa (L3682), and [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) holds the security master.
7. **Each side of an order has a test, and an error found in code that fed a quoted result, sent a paper or live order, or guards one reaches the owner in the same session.** Narang's first programming error reverses the signs, so a trader pays the spread he meant to earn (L4672). His AXA Rosenberg case is a risk model's coding error that ran from April 2007 to June 2009 and was then kept from the chief executive, and he says the firm whose execution server rebooted first had no warning of its error until it struck (L4672, L4690). Here a test starts from a forecast and runs through the one order path, gate and journal included, to the simulated broker, for a buy and a sell of each leg, each opening and closing, and an order that crosses zero and ends smaller than it started. It asserts the gate's classification of the order as adding or reducing, that position and cash move the way the forecast asked and that cost goes against the trader on both sides, and mutation shows a flipped sign fails it. The contract suite holds the same side assertion for each adapter. A session that finds such an error files it in that session, sends it through the alert path whatever the error cost, says whether the code is running, and labels the results it touched exploratory until they are recomputed on the fixed code, and a result from a registered sample regains its label only if rule 3 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) keeps that sample open. Where the error can change an order, a position or a recorded value, or keep a guard from stopping one, a session holding the operator's role on shadow and paper switches off the strategy running that code, under rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it). On a live account no session acts, and that rule leaves the switch-off open to any person.

### A result is measured against the risk it took, and size answers to what the owner can bear

Van K. Tharp's *Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom* (2nd ed., McGraw-Hill, 2007) supplies the rules for measuring a result against the risk it took and for choosing size. A citation like TYW L5720 is a Kindle location of a screen of the book's full text, and one like TYW L\~7086 is interpolated between the owner's highlights, good to about one screen. The book writes for people who trade their own accounts by hand, so what follows "Here" in a rule is this project's rather than Tharp's. Its figures are worked examples, one-off simulations or the author's experience, so none is adopted as a threshold, and its suggested percentages of risk set no size here.

These rules add no limit and no order, and they add no stop of their own apart from the case in item 4. Four things mark the edges of that statement.

1. Rule 3 and the drills below add to what a sample needs before it opens, and rule 3 to what closes one, as rule 2 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) does.
2. Rule 4 adds a step before the board sits on the Live step, and a proposal on size waits while the owner's figure is unwritten. Rule 5 adds a decision before a second strategy's first order on a shared account.
3. A report figure one of these rules cannot compute shows as missing on a report that still completes and alerts the owner, and that alone stops no trade. A cause another rule halts on still halts.
4. An adding order the gate passed with no finite positive worst loss journaled is an impossible case under rule 4 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs). It halts new orders and waits for the owner, cancelling nothing and keeping every working order, as an invalid stress move does under rule 1 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it), and [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) says what raises it.

This halt and the dead-man check's watch on the daily report have each fired in a drill before a sample opens on Paper. [#48](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/48) asks of each issue what these rules need from its build. Six rules follow.

1. **A position's result is read as a multiple of the worst loss the journal recorded for it.** Tharp states every profit and loss as a ratio of the total risk taken on the position, which he calls an R multiple, so results of different sizes compare (TYW L3664, L3810). He sets that risk with an exit fixed at entry, and holds that a trader without one has no system (TYW L5720). The slice 1 spread exits when its z-score crosses zero, so no price states that risk, and one of the book's worked examples takes the whole sum invested for a position with no preset risk (TYW L\~7086). He calls his own stand-in for a figure nobody recorded, the average loss, inaccurate (TYW L4094). Here the journal already holds a closer figure, so no report estimates one. Four things follow.
   1. A position's **initial risk** is the largest worst loss journaled on an order the gate passed and the platform sent or holds in doubt, from the order that takes the position off flat to the first point where every leg is flat by the derived position and no order is working or in doubt, which a fill, an outside entry or an order's settling can reach. It comes from the journal alone, or for a decision scored by replay from the worst loss its replay's gate computed. One such order with no finite positive worst loss journaled leaves that position's initial risk missing, never the largest of the rest. An add sent and never filled counts, because worst loss counts working and in-doubt orders, and a stress move written later restates no figure already journaled.
   2. A closed position's **R multiple** is its result after costs divided by its initial risk. An open position's divides its mark at recorded prices, net of the cost to close, by its initial risk so far. A before-cost figure divides the gross result by the same initial risk.
   3. The cost comes from the fills and the cost model version the decision's journal entry names, or from the sample's stated measure where one exists, under [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41). A charge the broker books with no fill counts as a cost once it is journaled, and until then the after-cost figure shows as missing. A broker fee or a dividend reaches the journal under [the ruling on #10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10#issuecomment-6674447318), and any other cash difference stays under rule 1 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer) and rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs).
   4. Every reader takes these figures and a position's boundaries from one calculation over the record, which [#48](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/48) asks [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) to name.

   The price is that a figure set by the stress move runs larger than the loss a usual exit takes, so multiples read small and compare with no example in the book, and a position that adds late reads a smaller multiple than its first unit earned. Adds tend to fall on losers in a mean-reverting position, which shrinks exactly the losses, so rule 2 shows a total beside the mean. The order and fill record cannot be recaptured, so [#48](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/48) item 1 asks for a test, before the first order, that the record is enough to rebuild both.
2. **A strategy's record is read as the whole distribution of its R multiples, with their count, their cost and the chances it had.** Tharp defines expectancy as the mean R multiple (TYW L1863). In his example of an active system, transaction costs take it from 0.5R to 0.4R (TYW L7774). He warns that the trades someone took can understate the chances a system offered (TYW L4001). He marks open positions at one day's close beside closed ones in his worked results (TYW L\~7372), and he only recommends counts of 30 and 100 trades (TYW L3862). Here a report that quotes **expectancy** states six things.
   1. It counts every position the sample opened, with one still open marked as item 2 of rule 2 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) says, and any closed-only figure beside it is labelled as one. A decision scored as rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) scores one keeps its place, with the worst loss its replay's gate computed, labelled as replay, under [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41). A decision rule 3 there reports apart stays apart and exploratory, with its count beside expectancy.
   2. It gives expectancy before and after cost.
   3. It gives a standard error beside the count of positions and the count of independent decisions [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) asks for, because positions held across days need not be independent. The standard error is this project's rather than Tharp's.
   4. It gives the largest loss in R and the count of results below -1R before cost. A result there is a loss past the journaled worst loss, which rule 4 of [A result is read against the luck that could have produced it](#a-result-is-read-against-the-luck-that-could-have-produced-it) reviews. A count of zero does not show that no such loss happened, because a position that took a gain on the way down can lose past its worst case and stay above -1R. These lines report closed results. Which record shows a position's loss beside its journaled worst loss, and what detects a move past the stress move, stays the question [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) item 7 asks of [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7).
   5. It counts requests by recorded outcome, so the counts sum to the requests made, in the one unit [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) states, which also says how a request with a leg in flight or in doubt counts.
   6. It gives the sample's total result after cost and its pooled figure, the sum of results over the sum of initial risks, because a mean of multiples can read positive while the account loses money.
3. **A strategy that opens a sample states how each position ends, and whether anything bounds it when that exit never fires.** Tharp asks a trader to know the worst-case exit at entry (TYW L5720). He asks a band trader, who buys at a lower band and sells at an upper one, what follows if the price never reaches a band, if the bands stop being accurate, if it moves the right way and stops short of the far band, and if it runs straight through (TYW L2291). This project reads the spread's exit at a z-score of zero as the first of those cases. The exit may not fire, worst loss covers only the stress move's window, and in slice 1 no halt unwinds a position apart from the close after a broker's trade. Here the specification that rule 2 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) commits also says how each position ends and the longest the strategy may hold one, or that it sets no such bound and why, under that rule's terms. A strategy with no sample open owes none of this. The daily report shows each open position's sessions held, beside that bound where one is stated, as [#48](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/48) asks of [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11). The rule adds no exit to any strategy. Passing a stated bound shows on the report and sends no order unless the strategy's own committed exit does, because a time limit changes the decision path and is judged as a variant.
4. **Size answers to a fall in the account the owner wrote down, and a proposal on a live size shows that fall at each size.** Tharp gives a system's objectives a large share of its design time (TYW L\~1197, L1590) and holds that position sizing is how a system meets them (TYW L1885). His arithmetic is short: an average drawdown of 30R at 0.5 percent risked per trade is a fall of about 15 percent, which he takes as the likely worst (TYW L8890). He compares sizing models on one breakout system, so only the size differs (TYW L8376). He warns that one list of R multiples is one ordering of them (TYW L8851), has a trader draw a year of results at least 100 times, and adds that a bigger loser than any seen is probably out there (TYW L4094). Here three things follow.
   1. **The owner's figure.** Before the board sits on the Live step, the owner writes the largest fall in the account's value that the owner accepts, as a share of the account's marked value at its peak, in the board's brief, which stays outside this repository, and records on the issue that proposes the step a dated line that the figure is written, carrying no value. That is a decision about capital, so this rule names it and sets no value. The figure is an input to a choice of size and no limit, and nothing stops trading at it unless that sitting adds a stop, which it decides whether to do, with the owner's ruling.
   2. **A proposal.** A proposal for a strategy's first size on a live account, a raise of size on one, or a change of the sizing model on one runs each candidate size as a full backtest, through portfolio construction and the one order path to the simulated broker, on data the strategy's research already read or a closed sample's sessions, and never on a held-out window or a sample still open. It shows at each size the worst fall in the account's marked value as a share of its peak, each position's worst loss as a share of the account's marked value, the orders refused and the targets the caps cut, so a size the caps stop never reads as a small fall. Seeded reorderings of the run's daily marked results, taken in blocks of consecutive sessions, with their count and length written before the run, show how far ordering alone moves that fall, as [#48](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/48) item 8 asks. The run is exploratory, each size tried counts as a try under rule 1 of [A result is read against the luck that could have produced it](#a-result-is-read-against-the-luck-that-could-have-produced-it), and it is no limit's worst case. The owner reads it against the figure, which no public surface carries, and while no figure is written the proposal waits. Rule 5 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) still governs any raise. A cut in size waits for none of this.
   3. **Each share's base.** In Tharp's tables, 5 percent of risk from a 21-day extreme sits near 1 percent of equity on 20-day volatility, so one size carries two percentages (TYW L8619). Here a share in a size, a limit, or a figure read against either names what it multiplies.
5. **The daily report shows each open position's worst loss and the account's fall from its peak, and a total once positions share an account.** Tharp sums the risk across his open positions (TYW L8619) and cites William Gallacher, who picks the largest drop he would bear and assumes it comes tomorrow (TYW L8802). The trader Tom Basso adds, in a note to another chapter, that markets stop moving independently in a crisis (TYW L4756). Here nothing adds the positions up, and nothing reports the fall already taken. So the daily report shows four things.
   1. Each open position's worst loss at the report's time, from the calculation the gate uses, as a share of the account's marked value.
   2. The fall of the account's marked value from its peak, as a share of the peak. The peak rebuilds from recorded values, cash the owner moves in or out is neither a gain nor a fall, and this report and a proposal under rule 4 read the fall from one calculation.
   3. Any position held past the stress move's window, which its worst loss does not cover, as rule 6 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) says.
   4. Each new closed result below -1R before cost, by name, so the review rule 2 sends it to starts from a named line.

   The report runs every session, halted or not, and the dead-man check watches its last run. In slice 1 one position needs no total. A second position in an account adds the total to the report. Before a second strategy's first order on a shared account, the owner decides whether a cap on that total joins the hard caps, as [#48](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/48) item 9 says, and no check on the order path reads that decision.
6. **A backtest quoted as evidence for an entry also shows what random entries earn with the same exit, size and cost.** Tharp separates entry from exits and sizing by running a coin flip through both. He reports that on 10 markets over 10 years his random-entry system profited on 80 percent of runs at one contract per market and on all of them at 1 percent risk (TYW L4834). He gives no count of runs and no median, and no comparison with what the market's own drift earned. The sibling [trading-strategies](https://github.com/l3a0/trading-strategies) repository ran a variant as exploratory work on a basket of its own. It held nine hand-picked ETFs and stocks, survivors only, from 2000-01-03 to 2026-06-30, on [settings it records](https://github.com/l3a0/trading-strategies/blob/4804cb2295502281374c03fccdf9d1d02c9d6e5e/engine/tharp_random_entry.py): 0.5 basis points a fill, 0.5 percent a year of borrow, and a 20-day simple average of true range where Tharp states a 10-day exponential one. It used [yfinance daily bars with dividends ignored](https://github.com/l3a0/trading-strategies/blob/4804cb2295502281374c03fccdf9d1d02c9d6e5e/docs/tharp_random_entry_plan.md), in one pinned setting over 100 seeds. Its log records an earlier 20-career scout of the same thesis on another engine and its plan names cost curves with no recorded result, so the full count of tries is unknown. [Its pinned test](https://github.com/l3a0/trading-strategies/blob/4804cb2295502281374c03fccdf9d1d02c9d6e5e/tests/test_tharp_random_entry.py) records 40 percent of careers ending above starting capital, a median career that lost, and the same average exposure with no timing, taken over the whole span with its own trading costs left out, beating the system in 98 percent of careers. That does not refute his futures result, and it is why the method is adopted and the claim is not. Here four things follow.
   1. A backtest quoted as evidence for an entry, on data the strategy's research already read and never on a held-out window or a registered sample's sessions, also shows seeded random entries through the quoted strategy's own exit, sizing and cost code and the one order path, gate included, to the simulated broker, with no module holding a branch for them.
   2. It reports the count of runs, the median, the share that made money, the quoted entry's rank among them, its trade count and time in the market beside theirs, and what the same exposure earns with no timing under the same fill rule, accounting and cost model, with no module holding a branch for it. The meaning of a random entry and of the same exposure, the count and the measure ranked are written before the run.
   3. These runs never reach a paper or live account, and a test shows a paper or live start cannot run them.
   4. As in rule 3 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails), they stay exploratory, and a result that kills an idea owes none of them.

### A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control

Larry Harris's *Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners* (Oxford University Press, 2003) supplies the rules for what a fill cost and for what a market can do to a position. A citation like TE L10544 is a Kindle location of a screen of the book's full text, and one like TE L\~14130 is interpolated between the owner's highlights, good to about one screen. The book describes the US markets of its day, and each "Here" in a rule marks where this project takes over from Harris. Its exchange rules, fees, venues and settlement cycle are dated and its figures are worked examples or data of that time, so none is adopted as a threshold or as a fact about today's market. A statement here about today's market, a broker or a venue comes from that party's published text and carries its date, as [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10#issuecomment-6512111773) records for Alpaca's cutoffs. The book never uses the words closing auction and mentions market-on-close orders, orders to be filled only at the session's close, only in passing, so nothing here says how a closing price forms.

These rules add no limit on an order or a position and no new kind of stop. They add one order, the close after a broker's trade under case 3 of rule 5. Three things mark the edges of the first statement.

1. Some rules add to what must happen before a step or to what closes a sample, and none adds a limit there.
   1. Rules 2 and 3 add to what a strategy's specification commits before a sample opens.
   2. Rule 4 adds to what a sample's registration names and what a Live proposal may cite, and rule 1 to what such a proposal shows and to the level and power a sample writes before it opens.
   3. Rule 5 adds a decision for the owner before a Paper sample opens, a bar on citing a Paper pass that the charter's Live stage states, a statement before the board sits on the Live step, and a second read of that statement's dated terms before the owner raises the capital cap. A maximum age raised above its value when the sample opened, or any such change to the check, closes that sample. Case 4 of rule 5 states both.
   4. Rule 7 adds to what the first limit order needs before it is sent.
   5. The drills below add to what a sample needs before it opens.
2. Rules 2 and 5 name inputs that meet hard limit 3's existing halt: a price of unstated kind, and a quote that fails rule 5's age and status check. Neither adds a kind of halt. Rule 5 also extends the existing halt on an order entry that names no snapshot to any order the gate read a price for, such as a person's, which has no decision. Seven things hold for each of the two halts on these inputs.
   1. Under the owner's rulings on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) [on what a halt keeps](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10#issuecomment-6622406689), [on a plan's own cancel](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10#issuecomment-6621789474) and [on a stored halt](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10#issuecomment-6672132028), the halt keeps every working order and the cancel that keeps a pair whole still goes out. A stored halt is written to storage, and a submit and a plan's cancel read it until a journaled release ends it. It stops a plan's own cancel and no other cancel. A plan's own cancel is one a plan makes to change or cancel its unfilled order, including the cancel ahead of a replacement. On shadow, paper and live each of these halts is a stored halt. One whose write failed or got no answer holds its own process as if stored, and no other, and that process writes no clean-shutdown record until the write lands. [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) says how.
   2. A cancel that rule 4 of [Keep the order path isolated](#keep-the-order-path-isolated) exempts skips the gate's check of its inputs. An order that only reduces the position stays held to every limit, the loss stop and any halt, apart from the stops case 3 of rule 5 names for the close after a broker's trade. A read of a quote, any other in-session price or a price kind that errors or fails its check, for any order and by any path, meets hard limit 3, and the halt holds that order like any other. Within that, [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) says what a limit that reads a price does for it when no valid price exists.
   3. What it refused, and every other value that read returned, is stored as it arrived, unparsed bytes included, as one snapshot, with the time the platform received it, before the halt. A write that fails or gets no answer still halts, under rule 5 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails).
   4. Its reason names the input and the defect, which rule 3 of [The code holds its own rules](#the-code-holds-its-own-rules-and-every-check-runs-in-production) checks with a test. It also names that snapshot, or for a read that returned nothing what was asked, when and the error. On shadow, paper and live the page names a failed or unanswered write of that snapshot or of the halt. The refused values reach storage, keeping their stamp and received time, before a journaled release ends that halt. Only where no copy survives does the release record their loss instead, and [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) says how. When the quote guard [#16](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/16) ports refuses a read for any cause, every row that read returned is stored and named the same way.
   5. It waits for the owner, or on shadow and paper the operator. A circuit breaker's own release, under rule 6 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs), ends only that breaker's halt, never one of these.
   6. A backtest, or a replay outside a sample, ends that run there, naming the halt, and where each session is its own run, as on [#16](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/16), the next session still starts. A result quoted across such runs states how many sessions a halt ended.
   7. A replay of a sample's sessions names the refused decision, gives it no default score and goes on. The count of refused decisions and their sessions stands beside the verdict, and [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) how the sample counts them.

   The price is that one refused read stops trading until a person clears it. A process that dies before a failed write lands can lose that halt and what only it held, whatever the cause. A failed write's page says so. The next start holds only under rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it), which a supervised restart does not trigger, and that restart re-reads its inputs, so trading can resume with no person having looked.
3. A report figure these rules cannot compute shows as missing on a report that still completes and alerts the owner, and that alone stops no trade. A cause another rule halts on still halts. In a backtest, a replay or shadow, a figure missing by construction shows why and alerts no one.

A broker's, fund's, venue's or data vendor's published text that a verdict, a citation of a pass or a line reading zero rests on, or that a sample's registration names, is saved as fetched, with its address and fetch time, outside this public repository, before a figure reads it. It counts as captured data wherever this doc says what must be kept or survive. [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) and [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) how.

Each of these halts, alerts and steps has fired in a drill before a sample opens on Paper. Case 3 of rule 5 explains the close, case 4 the age and status check, and case 5 a lone leg and the check of fills.

- The halt on a price of unstated kind.
- The halt on a quote that fails rule 5's age and status check.
- The halt on an order entry that names no snapshot.
- The page on a failed write of a snapshot or a halt.
- The close after a broker's trade, on a simulated broker's type whose contract case rests on that simulated broker's own recorded response, so the check rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) makes runs unchanged. No real broker's list holds that type, so it waits for the owner everywhere else, and a close built from a marked record carries the marker a real adapter refuses. The drill's report says no real broker's record has run, so it shows the path from the outside entry onward and nothing about recognising a real record.
- The page that no close can start, from two strategies holding the instrument.
- The page that a close waits for the owner's confirmation, once from an ordinary fill type with a recorded case and once from a type with no recorded case, and the close that confirmation starts. The drill's report says no real broker's record has run.
- The close with a fired loss stop or the retiring check's switch-off, which goes out, beside a decision's order the gate refuses.

- The alert on a missing close wait, by removing the value from shadow's configuration.
- The halt and alert on the count, once from an outside entry that can start a close whose close entry is withheld, once from a close entry whose outcome is withheld, and once from a page sent with its record withheld.

Three things bind each drill.

1. Its bad input, stopped check or removed value stays in shadow's marked loop under rule 6 of [Monitoring answers whether there is a problem first](#monitoring-answers-whether-there-is-a-problem-first), and a failed write's fault strikes only shadow's own stores.
2. Paper's own alert route is proved with no planted value entering Paper's records.
3. A session a drill halted is named as a drill, its records after the halt stay out of the comparison of shadow with a replay, and a quoted count of halted sessions names it apart.

[#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks of each issue what these rules need from its build. Seven rules follow.

1. **A fill's cost is measured from the quote its decision read, and never from the price the order was aimed at.** Harris compares what was traded with an imaginary portfolio traded at the decision, so a filled order's implementation shortfall is its size times the signed gap between its average trade price and the quote midpoint at the decision (TE L10431). A benchmark set after the order exists can be gamed. A broker judged on the close can trade only at the close and show no cost (TE L10544), and in his worked example the close gives a negative cost to a trader who paid the spread and moved the price (TE L10514). A benchmark fixed before the broker has the order cannot be gamed this way (TE L10544). He makes that point about brokers, so applying it to this platform's own orders is this project's step: an order that fills at that close scores about zero by construction, and the slippage monitor would never move. Here six things follow.
   1. **The cost line.** The slippage monitor and each figure that reports what a fill cost measure it from the midpoint of the quote in the snapshot its decision read, under rule 5 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails). It is per leg and signed so that a cost reads positive on either side. A backtest or an R multiple that charges the cost model's figure keeps it, and this line does not replace it. In a backtest or a replay a decision that read no quote shows this line as missing by construction, and any other order whose decision read no quote shows it as missing and alerts.
   2. **The commission line.** Commission and any fee the broker books on a fill stay outside the midpoint gap on a line of their own, because a broker's price for its services and its execution quality trade off (TE L12677), so a broker that charges no commission never reads as costing nothing. Harris speaks of a broker's price, so counting a booked fee here is this project's step. A fee the broker books apart from a fill never leaves that line reading zero, and [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) and [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) how it maps. That line reads zero only from a broker's record or its dated published fee text that states zero, and before that it shows as missing, with [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asking [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) which record settles it.
   3. **The check on the broker.** For a fill of an order meant to fill at the close, the gap between the fill and the close of the session's official bar, the vendor's daily bar [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) records, is a line of its own. It is never called a cost, for the reason above. It checks the broker against that bar, because Harris notes that brokers of his day would not guarantee a market-on-close order the closing price (TE L1949), and part of a gap can come from what that bar's close is, which rule 2's kind states. It is signed so that a price worse for the order reads positive on either side, per leg. The close is the price as traded that session, unadjusted for later splits. Where the bar is on another or an unstated basis, this line, the unfilled line, every other gap to that close and rule 6's share where it reads that bar's volume all show as missing. [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) proposes this line.
   4. **The unfilled line.** Failing to trade can cost more than trading (TE L10626), and Harris's shortfall counts an unfilled order too (TE L10431). So an order's unfilled quantity shows as that quantity times the move from the same midpoint to the close of the session's official bar, positive when the miss cost money. The line covers only the rest of that session, so it is no measure of the decision's result.
   5. **Each order's case.** Every quantity an order carries shows once, as filled, unfilled or working, and the quantity an order in doubt carries, or that a cancel in doubt covers, shows as missing, never as zero, until it settles. A request that sent no order shows neither the cost line nor the unfilled line. A quantity with no decision, filled or not, stays out of the average and the unfilled line and is named by what made it, such as an override, a contract-suite run or a close after a broker's trade. Rule 5 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) says which fills those are and halts on any other, and an outside entry journaled after that halt is named the same way, under rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs). [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#33](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/33) to map its outcomes onto these lines.
   6. **A simulated fill.** A cost measured on a simulated fill measures the simulator. A shadow fill follows [#16](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/16)'s rule, and [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10#issuecomment-6512111773) records, from Alpaca's paper trading page and its staff's forum replies looked up on 2026-10-29, that the paper account fills from quotes rather than simulating the closing auction. So no simulated fill calibrates the cost model. The price is that a pass cited for Live rests on a cost no real fill has tested. Two things follow.
      1. Any report or quotation of such a fill, its result or its cost labels it as simulated.
      2. Where a strategy sends market-on-close orders, a Live proposal citing a Paper pass names how its fills were priced, from the broker's dated text, and what they could not have reached, such as a closing auction. It shows beside the verdict, labelled exploratory, the gap from each fill to the close and the gap from the decision's midpoint to the close over the sample's sessions. Each gap is signed so that a price worse for the order reads positive on either side, shown per leg and as an average with its count of independent decisions and its standard error, and it reads as the paragraph after this list says, with the level and the power written before the sample opens. The verdict stays as computed, and whether to accept those gaps is the owner's. [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) how.

   The price is that a figure read from a decision at close−20, the time [#16](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/16) sets, carries the market's move over those twenty minutes and what the signal earns over them, so a difference from the model is not by itself slippage. That figure reads as an average with its count of independent decisions and its standard error. A gap a stated significance level does not separate from zero reads as not shown, never as the model holding, beside the smallest gap that count and standard error can detect at that level and a stated power. The level and the power are written before the first report shows the figure on a real fill or, for a gap a sample reads, before that sample opens. On the slippage monitor the gap is the average of cost minus the model's figure, and on the two gaps to the close it is the gap itself. How long a gap takes to read depends on how much of the market's move cancels across a decision's legs, which no recorded run has measured yet. [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) to compute that from recorded minutes, and how the monitor alerts under rule 2 of [Monitoring answers whether there is a problem first](#monitoring-answers-whether-there-is-a-problem-first).
2. **A recorded price states what kind of price it is, and a decision that reads two kinds says so.** A last sale can sit at the bid or the ask, so a series of them can bounce with no change in value (TE L10171), and a price fixed at one moment goes stale for what happens after it (TE L3131). Reported volumes also differ by how a market counts them (TE L1197). Here three things follow.
   1. Each market price the platform records or reads names its kind, such as the close of an official bar, a last sale or a quote midpoint, the venues it covers and any delay from its source, and each volume also names how it was counted. [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) where each field is read from. A later change in what a source means never changes what a value already recorded says it is.
   2. A field a source does not state is recorded as unstated and never assumed. A decision or the gate that would read a price of unstated kind meets hard limit 3, in every environment.
   3. A strategy's specification names the kind, coverage and delay of each price it was fitted on and decides on, and any mix among them. The daily report shows, for every strategy, the gap between the decision's midpoint and the close of the session's official bar, which [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) proposes as a monitoring line.
3. **A strategy's stated bet names who is on the other side, and whether anything forces the spread between its legs to close.** Profit in a zero-sum game has to come from someone who loses, so a trader needs to know why those people trade (TE L4183). Harris separates pure arbitrage, where the value of the two legs together strictly reverts to a mean, from speculative arbitrage, where it need not (TE L8519). One leg can move for good on news of its own, and a pair with no edge still bears the cost of opening and financing it (TE L8794). Here the specification that rule 2 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) commits also names who the strategy expects to trade against and why they accept the price. Its answer to why a link between instruments should hold says what forces the spread back, or that nothing does. Under [research pins](../CLAUDE.md#research-pins) these answers raise a prior and promote nothing. A strategy with no sample open owes none of this.
4. **A backtest quoted as evidence for a position held overnight or held short shows what holding it costs.** Holding a hedged position has carrying costs, among them interest paid or forgone and dividends paid on short positions (TE L8485), and a convergence slower than expected raises them (TE L9129). A short seller also pays a fee that depends on how scarce the security is, and the broker can earn interest on the short proceeds (TE L3653), which Harris says nearly all retail brokers kept from their clients (TE L3690). Here a backtest quoted as evidence, on data the strategy's research already read and never on a held-out window or a registered sample's sessions, states its holding terms and, from them, what the run's own positions cost to hold, with each term accruing on the days its source names. The terms and their sources are written before the run.
   1. The terms are whether the short could be borrowed on the run's dates, the borrow fee, any credit or charge on short proceeds and on a debit balance, and what a position receives or owes on a distribution, on each leg.
   2. Each term carries its source and date from the broker's or the fund's published text, and says whether the vintage's price basis already carries it.
   3. A term applied outside the dates its source covers says so.
   4. A term nobody has looked up shows as unknown and never as zero, and it leaves the holding cost unknown.

   Four things bound that figure.

   1. It sits beside the quoted result and stays exploratory, as the extra runs in rule 3 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) do. Adopting a holding cost because of it counts as a new variant.
   2. It leaves [#8](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/8)'s slice 1 cost as it is.
   3. A paper or shadow result quoted for such a position shows any term its simulator does not book as unknown, in the same way.
   4. A replication pin claims no edge, so it owes none of this.

   On a real account what the broker charges reaches the journal under rule 1 of [A result is measured against the risk it took](#a-result-is-measured-against-the-risk-it-took-and-size-answers-to-what-the-owner-can-bear). A sample and a Live proposal owe the same terms, in four steps, and [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) how.

   1. Before its sample opens, a sample's registration names which holding terms its measures count and, where one exists, the source of any it leaves out. Each source the registration names, for a term counted or left out, is saved with its date when the sample opens and when it ends.
   2. A term left out, or unknown, is named beside the verdict and never counts as zero, and the verdict stays as computed.
   3. A Live proposal cites that pass only if it still passes with each such term counted from the broker's or the fund's published text, dated and covering the sample's dates, labelled exploratory, a check that can withhold a pass and never make one.
   4. With no such source the term stays unknown, and the pass cannot be cited unless the owner accepts that term as unknown, in writing, named beside the verdict.
5. **A worst loss assumes a position can be held and can be left, and a broker or a venue can prevent either.** Worst loss reads the stress move over its window, as if the market stays open and the broker leaves the position alone. Six cases in Harris show how that can fail.
   1. **A short can be refused.** Shares to borrow are not always to be had (TE L\~404). Here the broker's refusal settles the order under rule 3 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer), and it is not resent. Case 5 covers a refused leg of a pair.
   2. **A short can be taken back.** A lender can demand its shares, and a short seller with no other loan must buy them back (TE L9150). Here a position change the recall causes at the broker matches no order the journal shows the platform sent, so rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) halts on it and cancels what that rule names. Where execution journals that change as a trade the broker made, or the owner confirms it as one, case 3's close follows. The platform sends no other order to repair it, and case 5 says when a person may.
   3. **A broker can close a position.** Arbitrageurs who cannot finance their losses may have to liquidate early (TE L9035), and in Harris's price-limit example the broker closes a position whose owner cannot meet margin calls (TE L\~14130). Here that fill also matches no order the journal shows the platform sent, so rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) halts on it and cancels what that rule names. Whatever the broker left has no exit, because its strategy stays off under rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it), and a pair left with one leg holds exposure no decision asked for. So reconciliation starts the close of the entire position, while the halt on that difference holds every other order. This **close after a broker's trade** is the one order the platform sends that no decision, override or contract-suite run stands behind. Seven things hold, and [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) how.
      1. **What starts it.** Outside entries for trades the broker made start it, once together they explain the whole difference, read under rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs), on every instrument the strategy holds, and no other strategy holds any instrument it holds. That rule says when execution may journal such an entry from the broker's record. A type that a platform's, a person's or another sender's order can also carry never qualifies there. The owner can supply what such a record lacks by confirming the trade as the broker's own, and that entry then starts the close on the same terms. A confirmation counts only where its entry names an **owner's record**, a create-only record for that account under a prefix only the owner's role may write on every account, as a release and an override are on a live account. That role takes a step of the person's own, one no agent session can reach, and what the step yields never sits where a session can read or use it. Every reader of a confirmation, the check of orders back to their causes and a release of a halt included, checks that record rather than the journal's row. A record that cannot be read counts as unreadable state, and a row that has no record or disagrees with its record halts and pages as a possible second writer. So a GitHub comment or a reply to a page is no confirmation. It names records the platform has read from the broker, with each trade's order, its strategy and its source, and its quantities come from those records and never from the confirmation. One naming records not yet read waits for them, and the page says so. Rule 5 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer) applies to an outside entry as to a fill, so each trade counts once across records and confirmations, and a repeat that differs is journaled beside the first, starts nothing and halts. A confirmation of any other source starts nothing. One that fails these terms starts nothing and pages, and a corrected one can follow. Otherwise it stands for that stretch, so the first run that meets these terms starts the close. Nothing else starts the close: not a position read alone, whatever it shows, not a read rule 2 cannot judge, not a split, not a difference that still waits for the owner, and not a lone leg with any other cause. So a missing input can never read as a broker's trade, and hard limit 3 stands. An entry is able to start a close when rule 2 accepts its type or an owner's record confirms it as the broker's own, whatever the other terms show, and a confirmation counts from its owner's record's stored stamp, less the clock's bound, whether or not the platform has read its broker records yet. The halt's page says whether a close has started, cannot start and why, or has not started yet and what would start it. Before the first outside entry able to start a close, that page says a switch-off made now declines nothing. Where the owner's confirmation would start it, the page says what that would close, that the order may be a second sender's, and what to check outside the platform. It names any halt that would still hold the close, and pages again when that changes, and when the first entry able to start a close is journaled, saying a switch-off now declines the close.
      2. **The one record.** Execution journals one **close entry**, naming the outside entries, each leg, the strategy's quantity in the book and a target of flat for each leg. The store allows one for each stretch the strategy stays off, in a key that leaves out the lease number on purpose. So a restart, a lease takeover, a second run of reconciliation or a later outside entry starts nothing new, while a position reopened after a person's switch-on can be closed again. The store also allows one order entry and one outcome for each leg, in the same write. A switch-on is refused while any leg has no outcome or its order is unsettled or still working, or while an entry is owed, and the flat target and the gate's reading of the entry end with the stretch. An outside entry able to start a close stays owed until a close entry or a journaled refusal stands for its stretch. A refusal covers only a closed list: an entry the owner confirmed as another source, or another strategy holding an instrument this one holds. It is final for that stretch and pages that none can start. The store takes a refusal or a close entry that can send for a stretch, never both, in the same write. Any other term that falls short, a read that fails or does not reach the store included, leaves the entry owed, so a switch-on and the release of the halt on that difference stay refused, and past the written wait the page says so. A person's switch-off is the way to decline the close, an owed one included, so execution journals the close entry marked as declined, every leg ends as held and the gate lets no order through under it. The gate and rule 3's check do not judge the moving terms again. They check that the close entry names at least one outside entry, that every one carries, from the broker's message as journaled on receipt, a type rule 2 accepts as the broker's own, or has an owner's record confirming it as the broker's own, that each belongs to this stretch and strategy, and that the close entry is not marked as declined.
      3. **What it closes.** Rule 2's test now reads each leg against flat, so it cancels any order with a target whose fill would take the leg away from flat or past it, the close's own included, and keeps the rest. A leg waits while any order or cancel on it has no settled answer. Each close covers only what the kept orders leave of the strategy's quantity, including the rest of a leg the broker closed in part, so the kept orders and the close together land the leg at flat unless the broker trades it again. Kept orders that add to the leg, such as an override's, or that together would pass flat stop that leg under part 6. Just before each send, execution reads the broker's position and open orders on that leg and sends only if they match the book.
      4. **How it goes out.** Each close is a new order on the one order path, journaled before it is sent, and it passes the gate held to every limit and every stop but those part 5 names. The gate reads the close entry, and any mark on the order grants nothing. It lets through only execution's own close, under an entry that passes part 2's check, as one order for that leg, on the closing side. It refuses a close whose size with every working or unanswered order on that leg would pass flat, and the part that crosses flat counts as adding. [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) says how the net cap reads the legs of one close. A reconciliation during a start sends nothing, so the close goes through the order path after Startup's checks. [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) says its order type, the order of the legs, and what it does past a cutoff or outside a session.
      5. **What holds it.** These stops do not hold it: the halt on the difference those entries explain, the switch-off the outside entry itself raises, a fired loss stop, and the switch-off the retiring check's own rule raises. The last two are among them because the close only reduces each leg, and a forced close and a large loss can arrive on the same day. The gate and the read of the halt state just before the send take the first two from the journal, because the halt names the instruments and quantities it covers and any further difference is another halt. The last two stand aside only for an order under a valid close entry. The kill switch, any other halt and unreadable state hold the close, and a loss stop's state, or a switch-off's source, that cannot be read counts as unreadable. That includes the halt in rule 3 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) on an order that traces to nothing, which fires first where the broker's order shows before its record, because it guards against a second sender. That halt does not hold a close the owner's confirmation started, for the orders the confirmation names, and it stands against everything else until the owner releases it. The start, the gate and the read before a send compare the halt's order ids with that record, and an id missing or unreadable holds the close. A person's switch-off journaled after the stretch's first outside entry able to start a close and before the send also holds the close, even on a strategy already off. An older switch-off declines nothing, because no close existed to decline, and neither does one rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) raised before that entry. Any other stop a rule raises counts as any other halt, a retiring check that has not completed in its written time included. While any of these but a person's switch-off stands, execution journals no close entry. It pages, naming the stop and saying its release starts orders unless a person switches the strategy off first, and the first run after the stop ends starts the close, which the check of orders back to their causes reads as in flight until then. A person's switch-off is accepted wherever the journal can take the write, holds only once journaled, and tells the person when the write failed or got no answer. Execution then journals the close entry under any stop, with every leg held. No one releases the halt on that difference, on any environment, before every leg has its outcome and its recorded page with its order settled under rule 3 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer) and stopped working at the broker, and on a live account only the owner clears it then.
      6. **When a leg stays open.** Each leg gets one try and one journaled outcome, and a further entry completes that outcome where its order was still working or in doubt when the leg ended. A hold that arrives before the send ends the try. Every outcome pages the owner. One that leaves the broker's leg away from flat also names the position, its net exposure, any order still working or in doubt, what stopped the close and the owner's step, and the platform never sends that leg again. Case 5's alert stands. A wait past a written time ends the leg, and with no valid time the leg ends before its send. That time runs from the close entry's journal stamp with the clock's bound added, so it ends sooner, and a restart or a lease takeover never resets it. A change to it is reviewed like a change to a limit, and a wait does not stall reconciliation's runs. After a restart or a lease takeover, a leg with an outcome is never sent, a leg with an order entry and no outcome settles as an order in doubt, and a leg with neither goes on only if the journal shows no hold in force at the close entry or raised since it, and otherwise ends as a held leg. The read just before each send applies the same test. A close entry, a refusal or an outcome with no page pages, and a close entry's page goes out before any leg is sent. A page that fails or gets no answer sends no leg, which then ends under its wait, and the check below counts it as a gap. The check of orders back to their causes counts outside entries that met every term on a recorded run, from that run's stored reads, against close entries and refusals, each close entry not marked as declined and each refusal against a recorded run whose stored reads support it and came first, a declined entry against a person's switch-off journaled after the stretch's first outside entry able to start a close, legs against outcomes, and close entries, refusals and outcomes against recorded pages. A gap past the written wait, counted from the later of the record or recorded run it follows and the end of any stop that held the start, halts, names the missing record where the halt-state monitor reads it, alerts, and waits for the owner, or on shadow and paper the operator. A missing or invalid wait counts as a gap, so the count halts rather than reading as in flight.
      7. **What it leaves.** The strategy stays off until a person switches it back on, and a retired strategy also needs its new registration. The owner gets a page when the close entry is journaled, naming the legs and quantities and saying a person's switch-off stops a leg not yet sent, which can leave net exposure wider once another leg has filled, and another when the last leg ends, which says the next decision after a switch-on reopens the full target and, for a retired strategy, that it needs a new registration first. The daily report names the broker's trade and each close, and the halt-state monitor shows a close running until its last leg ends.

      The price is ninefold.

      1. The close takes whatever the market gives and gives up any recovery the position would have had.
      2. On a live account, a close from a record whose type marks the trade is an order no person saw before it went out.
      3. The loss stop and the retiring check's switch-off do not hold it, so orders go out on a day past that stop and for a strategy its own rule has retired, and a leg that stops there can leave net exposure wider, as the next item says.
      4. A leg that stops after another has filled can leave net exposure wider than the broker left it, until a person acts, with the net cap still bounding it.
      5. A broker still selling can carry a leg past flat, up to that leg's size. The further difference halts and the leg waits for a person, because the close entry starts nothing new.
      6. A broker's trade whose type does not mark it as the broker's own, with or without a recorded case, reaches the owner, and the close waits for the owner's confirmation. The legs stay open for as long as that takes. A confirmation given in error closes the position, and where a second sender's order caused the trade, its key stays live. As of 2026-11-06, Alpaca's [account activities page](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/account-activities), updated 2026-10-16, gives a fill no type or field that marks it as the broker's own, and its only assignment type is for options. So until a recorded response from Alpaca shows such a mark, every forced close there takes that step.
      7. Where the broker's order shows before its record, the halt on an order that traces to nothing holds the close until the owner confirms the trade or releases that halt. Which arrives first at a broker is unmeasured. At Alpaca, where no record marks the trade, that halt fires wherever the broker's order shows.
      8. A stop just before the close entry defers the close, and the same stop just after it ends the try, so one write decides whether a leg stays open.
      9. A switch-off declines only between the first outside entry able to start a close and the send, or while a stop defers the close, so a strategy switched off earlier still closes and a record whose type marks the trade leaves no way to decline in advance. A decline while an entry is owed forfeits the close, even if the read returns a minute later.
   4. **A market can stop.** In Harris's futures example a position sits through days at a price limit (TE L\~14130), and US equities stayed shut from 2001-09-11 to 2001-09-17 (TE L\~14362). A position held past the stress move's window already shows on the daily report under rule 5 of [A result is measured against the risk it took](#a-result-is-measured-against-the-risk-it-took-and-size-answers-to-what-the-owner-can-bear). Here this project assumes a halted market can leave its last quote readable, which no recorded halt has shown, so a decision could take a frozen quote, one that has stopped updating, for a current one. The quote guard [#16](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/16) ports from [l3a0/quantitative-trading](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/commit/487bf117) reads no quote's age, as a hand-built row showed on 2026-11-05, recorded on [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50). Four things hold, and [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#5](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/5) and [#16](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/16) to state the check before the first decision reads a quote.
      1. A decision or the gate reads a quote, or any other in-session price, only under the maximum age its strategy writes for that source, measured on the source's own stamp. Each strategy writes its own in tracked configuration, because how old a price can be and still serve depends on how the strategy uses it, and a person's order reads the tightest maximum among the strategies whose positions it touches. An order that touches none reads no written maximum, so it is refused under hard limit 3, and that never blocks a cancel or the kill switch. A raised maximum counts as a raise of a limit under rule 3 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it). So does any other change to the check that lets through a read it refused when the sample opened, such as what counts as a usable stamp, a stated delay or a normal status. A lowered maximum counts as a tightened limit. Rule 2 of [Time comes from one clock](#time-comes-from-one-clock-and-order-from-sequence-numbers) adds the clock's bound to that age. This maximum, the wait in case 3, and the time and the bound in case 5, count as missing when any is not a finite positive number.
      2. A quote or other in-session price past that age, one with no usable stamp, one marked delayed or of unstated delay, one whose status is missing or not normal where its source publishes one, or a read with no written maximum is stale under hard limit 3.
      3. The price, its stamp, any status and the time the platform received it, for what a decision or the gate read or a halt refused, sit in a snapshot, under rule 5 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails). An order the gate read such a price for goes out only with that snapshot stored and named, and an entry that names none halts the same way that rule's does. [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) says how the entry names it and keys it.
      4. A frozen quote passes when neither its stamp nor its status shows it, and nothing here detects that. Before a Paper sample opens the owner closes that gap or accepts it, having seen how many sessions the record covers, each recorded real halt on the source the decision reads, how each was identified other than by the quote's own status, and how long each ran against that strategy's written maximum. A count of none reads as not shown, never as no frozen quote. The charter's [Live stage](team-charter.md#5-release) says what must happen to it before a sample's pass can be cited for Live.
   5. **A pair can end with one leg.** Failure to execute is especially serious once one leg has traded (TE L8985). Here a **lone leg** is a position whose legs, by fills and the outside entries assigned to it, leave a net exposure its decision did not ask for. While a reconciliation mismatch on the position stands, its legs read as the broker reports them. Five things hold, and [#50](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/50) asks [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11) how.
      1. The gate already counts every working buy filled with no sell and the reverse, under rule 2 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer). [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10) holds what execution does with a lone leg, and [PR #15](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/pull/15) proposes keeping a pair whole.
      2. The daily report names a lone leg and its net exposure.
      3. The check of fills against positions under rule 2 of [Faults are expected](#faults-are-expected-and-the-platform-checks-itself-while-it-runs) alerts the owner, halted or not, when one still stands a written time after the first fill, entry or broker read that showed it, or at that check's last run of the day if sooner, whether its partner is working, in doubt or ended, and neither a later fill nor a restart resets that time. A change to that time is reviewed like a change to a limit, and a check with no written time alerts rather than passing. The alert names the position, its net exposure and each order on it still working or in doubt, and while a halt holds it, the owner's step at the broker.
      4. Monitoring's own alarm reads the age of that check's last completed run, halted or not, and pages the owner past a written bound. A missing bound alerts too, and a last run that is absent or unreadable reads as past that bound. A change to that bound is reviewed like a change to a limit. That is the route [#11](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/11#issuecomment-6675471429) gives the jobs it names. On that route monitoring reads that age only when a scheduled entry runs, whether it makes a decision or only reports in, so silence can reach the owner up to a day late, or on Monday for a check that stopped on Friday. That is the accepted price for this check too, because the daily report still names a lone leg and the dead-man check pages when the machine or monitoring is dead.
      5. The platform sends no order of its own for a lone leg outside a decision, apart from case 3's close. An order a person places through the gate may act on a lone leg once no halt holds that order and no order in doubt blocks its instrument, under rule 4 of [Keep the order path isolated](#keep-the-order-path-isolated) and rule 3 of [Every fact has one writer](#every-fact-has-one-writer). Once [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) adds an unwind, [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) says when the unwind may act on it. In slice 1, where case 3's close does not reach it, while a halt holds and no partner is working or in doubt, only the owner's act bounds the position, under rule 6 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it).
   6. **A broker can fail.** Clients who deposited assets with a broker that goes bankrupt risk losing them (TE L4080).

   So before the board sits on the Live step, the risk engineer states for each strategy, from the broker's and the venue's published text with its date, whichever of these apply.

   - The borrow and recall terms.
   - The margin the stress move would demand per unit of the capital cap, and whether the broker could close the position first.
   - The halt rules for its instruments.
   - What protects the account's assets if the broker fails.
   - How the broker marks a trade it makes itself, and so which of its trades can start case 3's close with no person's act and which wait for the owner's confirmation.

   The statement goes on the issue that proposes the step, carries no dollar figure, and has its dated terms read again before the owner raises the capital cap. How much of this exposure to accept is the owner's decision, and the statement sets no value of capital.
6. **The platform's share of each instrument's volume is reported, and nothing here manages execution at this size.** Harris says that showing an order to the market rarely costs a small trader, whose orders are too small to draw traders who profit from seeing them, though many small orders on one side can add up to a large one (TE L9418). Large orders are harder to execute than small ones (TE L1672). Here [#8](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/8)'s slice 1 cost reads order size but not the market's volume, so the daily report shows the platform's filled, working and in-doubt quantity in each instrument, on each side, as a share of the session's volume. No value yet says where that cost stops holding. A fitted cost model states the sizes rule 5 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded, and each model states how it fails](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) names, and what share of volume those sizes were. The rejected register holds why nothing here splits, randomizes or hides an order. The price is that the platform gives away what a large trader would protect.
7. **A simulated resting order names what it assumes about adverse selection, and what it missed shows.** Standing limit orders give others a free option to trade (TE L238). Traders who take that option subject limit orders to adverse selection, so such an order tends to trade when its owner would rather have moved it and to miss the trades its owner wanted (TE L7363). Here nothing is built until the first limit order, and before it is sent [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10)'s fill rule for it states what it assumes about a market price that touches the limit and trades no further. That assumption and any difference from the real broker are named as gaps in the contract suite, and what stayed unfilled shows under rule 1.

### A search is written down before it runs, and a setting is chosen with its neighbours

Robert Pardo's *The Evaluation and Optimization of Trading Strategies* (2nd ed., Wiley, 2008) supplies the rules for searching a strategy's settings and for reading what the search found. A citation like EO L4564 is a Kindle location of a screen of the book's full text, and one like EO L\~5122 is interpolated between the owner's highlights, good to about one screen. The book writes for traders who build systems on the retail platforms of its day, and each "Here" in a rule marks where this project takes over from Pardo. Its figures come from the author's experience and from research he does not cite. He takes a walk-forward efficiency above 50 or 60 percent as passing, crediting that research (EO L4672). He calls the traditional minimum of 30 trades rather small (EO L4300), and elsewhere takes 30 to 50 trades as an adequate minimum, with at least 90 percent of a test's degrees of freedom remaining (EO L5810). So none of its figures is adopted as a pass mark. These rules add no stop. They bind the research that leads to a registration, which [the sample rules](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) then judge.

1. **A search is written down before it runs, ranks by a measure written down with it, and keeps every setting's result.** Pardo calls the set of simulations a search produces, one for each setting, its optimization profile (EO L4415). He warns that an unsound measure for ranking them can promote overfitting (EO L3946), and net profit alone is one, because it ignores the size of the trade sample and the risk taken (EO L3987). The count of settings is the product of each range's steps (EO L3598). Here a search's record names, before its first result exists, the parameters it varies, each range and step, the ranking measure with any floor it applies, such as a least number of trades, what counts as a setting that did well, the data it reads, which never includes a held-out window or a registered sample's sessions, and the false-discovery-rate control the [research pins](../CLAUDE.md#research-pins) require of a search over many variants. After the run it keeps the result of every setting it evaluated, not only the best. The count it evaluated is part of the count of tries that rule 1 of [A result is read against the luck that could have produced it](#a-result-is-read-against-the-luck-that-could-have-produced-it) asks every quoted result to state. A change to any of these after any result exists is a new search with a record of its own. [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) holds what the result record carries and how a count of tries is kept.
2. **A setting is chosen by a rule, written down before the search, that scores it together with its neighbours.** Pardo prefers a setting that sits among friendly and profitable neighbors to a profit spike, which he calls typically a statistical anomaly and among the commonest causes of overfitting (EO L4564). Rule 3 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) already shows the settings on each side of the chosen one. Here they also decide the choice: the rule that picks a setting scores each one with those around it, and a record that quotes a setting shows beside it the setting that ranks first on the ranking measure alone, before neighbours are scored, when the two differ. A setting the rule written beforehand picks is not a new variant, and a pick moved after the neighbouring figures were read is one. Pardo reads the share of settings that profit as a sign of whether a result is chance (EO L4461), and he puts no measured chance level behind it. Here a record that quotes that share also quotes where it ranks among the shares of the same grid run with no edge through the same trading, sizing and cost code, a number of times written before the run, with their trade count beside the grid's, and no share is a pass mark. [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) holds how that score is recorded, and [#46](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/46) holds how a backtest shows the neighbouring settings.
3. **A backtest result states the standard error of its average trade, and the share of its sample its lookback consumed.** Pardo computes the standard error of an average from its spread over the square root of the count (EO L2709). He holds that a test needs enough degrees of freedom, and that a long lookback on a slow strategy consumes them heavily (EO L2748, EO L5810). Rule 3 of [A backtest names what it could not have traded](#a-backtest-names-what-it-could-not-have-traded-and-each-model-states-how-it-fails) already has a sample's report count its fitted settings beside its independent decisions. Here a backtest result quoted for a strategy also states its longest lookback and the share of the sample that lookback used before the first decision. Its standard error of the average trade names the count it divides by and how that count was formed, because a pair's legs and positions held across days need not be independent, and [#44](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/44) holds how independent decisions are counted. [#41](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/41) holds what a sample's report gives.
4. **A walk-forward is exploratory here, reports its parts, and counts its window sizes and its reruns as tries.** Pardo judges a strategy only on its trading after each optimization, on data the optimization never saw (EO L4631), and reads the ratio of that profit to the profit inside the optimization as walk-forward efficiency (EO L4672). He calls the two window sizes two more variables of the strategy (EO L4269), and lets a failed walk-forward be reviewed for error and run again (EO L5010). The owner ruled on 2026-11-02 on [#4](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/4#issuecomment-6612511490) that backtest runs no registered experiment, so a walk-forward's result is exploratory and never a verdict. Neither its optimizations nor the windows after them read a registered sample's sessions or a held-out window, which the [research pins](../CLAUDE.md#research-pins) hold back. Here a walk-forward reports profit inside and after the optimizations, annualized and after costs, their ratio, the count of windows and how widely they differ, and never the ratio alone. Its window sizes count among the settings tried, and each run after a failed one counts as another try. Pardo re-optimizes a traded strategy on a schedule and reads the window after each optimization as how long a set of settings stays good (EO L4760). A strategy that re-fits its own settings while it trades changes its decision path under rule 3 of the sample rules, and whether a schedule written before a sample could itself be the path that sample registers is the owner's to decide when a strategy first proposes one. Whatever the owner rules on that, a re-fit changes only the parameters its schedule names, and never the strategy's size rule, the longest it may hold a position, what its input check accepts, such as a written maximum age, a hard cap or any other limit it trades under, whose raise closes a sample under rule 3 of the sample rules.
5. **A rule added after reading a result names what prompted it, and shows its effect on the data that did not prompt it.** Pardo's example adds a stop after one large losing trade (EO L5584), and his forecasting and trading cases each fit the data already seen more closely and then do worse out of sample, the trading one losing $15,000 over six months of testing out of sample (EO L5647, EO L5705). Rule 1 of the luck rules already counts such a change made during research as one more variant, and rule 3 of the sample rules counts one made while a sample is open. Here its record also names the result that prompted it, and shows the change on the periods and instruments that result did not touch, beside its effect on the ones it did. Both runs use data its research already read, never a held-out window or a registered sample's sessions, and are labelled exploratory. The record may follow the change, and it holds back no change that cuts risk, such as a tightened limit, an added stop, or anything rule 3 of the sample rules or rule 5 of the luck rules says is never delayed.

### Data has a cost, except the data that cannot be recaptured

1. **Every class of derived data has an owner and an end date.** Data costs more than its storage, and the book lists six costs that grow with it (AoS L8947). Most of its value falls with age, so keeping it forever needs a stated value and a time limit (AoS L8963, L9168). Diagnostic logs, intermediate backtest output and feature tables follow that rule. Derived data is rebuilt by a command rather than repaired by hand, since DDIA counts tools that recompute data among the ways to recover from human error (DDIA L391). Recorded data is exempt as a class: captured data, every snapshot a decision or the gate read or a halt refused, every published text a verdict, a citation or a line reading zero rests on or a sample's registration names, as the platform saved it, every split record, the journal, every backup of any of them, and every broker statement the platform saved. No lifecycle or cleanup rule may match a path that holds it. The operator's change log and incident evidence are kept as long as the operator sets on purpose, rather than by this rule. A legal duty to delete, such as a vendor's license terms, is the one exception, and it is a written procedure the owner carries out by hand, naming every copy it must reach, never a rule that runs on its own. The procedure appends a record of what it removed, which the read-back and the restore drill treat as expected. DDIA notes that immutable stores and backups make deletion hard, and that collected data is a hazard to keep only while it is used (DDIA L\~14509, L17128).

## How work is cut and ordered

A slice is the smallest set of deliverables that leaves the product usable by
someone at the end of it. Not a layer, and not a subsystem. A slice that ends
with a component nobody can run is a slice cut the wrong way.

Each slice lives on the tracker as three things.

1. A milestone with the slice's name, so a filed issue has one to take.
2. A parent issue holding what the slice leaves usable and the tests that show
   it works, each stated as what a test executes rather than what the code
   looks like.
3. One sub-issue per deliverable.

A sub-issue that cannot start until another finishes gets a "blocked by" link
to it, and a slice's parent issue that waits on another slice gets one to that
slice's parent issue. Name only the dependencies that are real. An invented
dependency is how a plan quietly becomes a layered build.

Order the slices by what the ranking rule in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)
decides: what is missing from the shortest path to a product someone can use
goes first. Then ship it, use it, and let what breaks set the order after
that. Slice 1 is the walking skeleton, on [#4](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/4). The next slice is chosen
by what breaks while the skeleton runs on paper.

Steve McConnell's *Rapid Development* (Microsoft Press, 1996) supplies the
rules for estimating, cutting and tracking the work, cited by printed page,
so RD p. 199 is page 199. The book writes from studies of human teams in the
1980s and 1990s, so its rules are adopted for their method rather than their
numbers, and how well they fit agent loops is untested. The estimate and
actual pairs rule 2 starts are that test. Its chapters on motivation,
overtime and team structure have no counterpart for agent sessions and are
not adopted. Six rules follow.

1. **A slice builds its most important behavior first, and its usable end
   includes every mechanism the design principles build from a path's first
   run.** McConnell's staged delivery ends every stage with a product that
   meets its size, performance and quality targets as well as its function,
   and delivers capabilities from most to least important (RD p. 553). A
   working product is a more accurate status report than any paper report
   (RD p. 551). Here the order inside a slice is the build board's, set by
   its band labels, its "blocked by" links and the parent issue's
   `Board order:` line, and a slice is usable when the walking skeleton's
   smoke test reaches every module it added with those mechanisms in place.
2. **An estimate is a range drawn from loops of the same kind, posted under
   its own heading, and the next estimate of that kind carries the slip.**
   McConnell's multipliers, adapted from Boehm and others' COCOMO 2.0 of
   1995, put an estimate of effort made at the first concept anywhere from a
   quarter to four times the final figure (RD p. 169). Estimating from memory
   of similar projects is the commonest practice and goes with overruns
   (RD p. 178, citing Lederer and Prasad 1992). So a build issue carries a
   comment opening with `## Estimate` that gives a range in dollars and names
   the loops it was drawn from, which are earlier loops of the same kind, such
   as module builds or review rounds, with the same coverage as the
   `## Cost` figures they are compared with. Where no loop of that kind has
   posted a cost, the first estimate is a labelled guess inside the widest of
   McConnell's ranges. A survey of more than 300 projects found that a
   project behind schedule hardly ever makes up the lost time
   (RD p. 199, citing van Genuchten 1991), and McConnell recalibrates by
   raising what remains by the share of the slip so far (RD p. 381). So when
   a loop's `## Cost` lands above its range, the next estimate of that kind
   raises its range by the same ratio. A later estimate is a new
   `## Estimate` comment rather than an edit.
3. **A loop that runs past its estimate drops behavior from a list written in
   advance, and never a check.** McConnell's timebox drops features rather
   than quality, and he advises against boxing planning, requirements and
   design, whose mistakes cost the most downstream (RD p. 581). Here a build
   issue lists, in order, the behaviors a slip may drop. That list never
   holds anything the ranking rule's three exceptions name, a hard limit, the
   risk gate or any of its limits, a halt, the loss stop, the kill switch, a
   rollback, an alarm or the drill that shows it works, a review, a test, or a
   part the design fixes from the first commit, such as the partition key and
   count or the event and clock interface. A dropped behavior moves to an
   issue of its own, and its acceptance tests stay in the suite as strict
   expected failures that name that issue. A cut that touches the order
   path, the gate or its limits goes to the risk lens before it takes
   effect, and the risk engineer may veto it. A slice whose must-have behavior does not fit goes back to
   planning whole.
4. **Done is the charter's Definition of done, and never a percentage.**
   McConnell's test of a finished piece is whether it could be locked in a
   vault and shipped as it stands, so a piece "99 percent done" is not done
   (RD p. 381). A report of done links the merged pull request that closed
   the issue, its `## Review` comment with the mutation result, and the
   checks that ran the changed code. A check that never ran the changed code
   shows nothing.
5. **Each slice keeps a list of the risks to its delivery, and a risk to
   something that cannot be undone ranks above every one that can.**
   McConnell's top-10 risks list names the risks to a project's schedule and
   gives each its rank this week and last, its weeks on the list and the
   progress on resolving it (RD p. 85, p. 100 to p. 102). He ranks risks by
   probability times size, then moves a larger loss above its place in that
   order (RD p. 95, p. 96). Here a risk that could lose one of the premise's
   three things ranks above every other, ordered among themselves by the risk
   engineer's severity and detection scores, which still decide splits and
   detectors for each change. The list ranks what to watch, never what to
   build next, which the ranking rule in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md) decides.
   It sits on the slice's parent issue under a `## Risks` heading. The
   status round reviews it whenever it names a next card from the slice,
   under the `/l3a0:sync-prune-next` skill's step for it, and posts a dated
   comment opening with `## Risks review` even when nothing changed, so the
   reviews can be counted. The round reports a slice with no list rather
   than writing one.
6. **A spike answers one stated question, is exploratory, and never becomes
   production code, and shared code waits for a design that names it.**
   McConnell treats each throwaway prototype as an experiment with a
   hypothesis, and stops it once the hypothesis is settled (RD p. 572).
   Prototyping is exploratory by nature, so a spike's result is
   [exploratory](#vocabulary) whatever it stated in advance. A spike lives
   in a top-level `spikes/` folder that no module under `src/` imports. It
   reads data that cannot be recaptured only through copies, never reads the
   holdout window, sends no order, paper included, and holds no broker
   credential. A question about a strategy's returns goes through the
   engine's backtest, never a spike. A spike that kills an idea is pinned
   under CLAUDE.md's research pins rather than deleted. A reusable component
   costs two to three times as much as a one-off and breaks even only after
   two or three uses, McConnell citing Biggerstaff's rule of three, so his
   rule of two builds a component as a one-off first and considers making it
   reusable at its second use (RD p. 535, p. 536, citing Tracz 1995). The
   shared types and interfaces that
   [#22](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/22) lands before
   any module are a design named ahead of use, not reuse. Code the sibling
   repositories keep using moves to
   [ithildin-core](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-core) and is depended on
   at a pinned version, and code salvaged from a sibling is only code that
   sibling retires (RD p. 529).

## Vocabulary

Terms with exact definitions, reused on purpose. A term listed here is not a
candidate for a synonym. Anything not listed here follows the writing-style
rule in [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md), which prefers deleting an in-group term to
glossing it.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **vintage** | One series as one source held it on one date, recorded with vendor, symbol, span, that date and which price the series carries, and kept unchanged once written. The term and its rules come from the sibling quantitative-trading repository |
| **target portfolio** | The positions portfolio construction asks for. The difference between it and the current positions is what execution trades |
| **risk gate** | The pre-trade check every order passes on the one order path. It enforces the risk engineer's limits and can refuse an order |
| **journal** | The append-only record of every intent, order, acknowledgment, fill, cancel, lease, halt, outside entry, human override, contract-suite run, close entry and each close leg's outcome, written before the action it records. It also records each adding order's worst loss, or the term that left it uncomputable, each request's recorded outcomes with their reasons, each drill before it starts, each opening, pause or end of a strategy's sample with its cause, each clean shutdown, and each confirmation after a stop without one |
| **order path** | The path from the target portfolio through the risk gate and the journal to the broker adapter. Its synchronous dependencies are the calls on it that leave the process or wait on input or output |
| **written scale limit** | The most load an implementation handles as built, stated beside the code that enforces it, as distinct from the most its design allows |
| **lease number** | The number each grant of an account's lease carries, higher than every earlier grant and every number the journal or the broker's client order ids hold, recorded in every journal entry and client order id. The journal's store refuses a write that names an older lease |
| **event time** | When the source says an event happened, as the source's own timestamp states it |
| **receive time** | When capture received a record, read from the shared clock |
| **send time** | When the source says it sent a message, on the source's clock, where the feed carries one |
| **receive sequence** | The gap-free number capture gives each record in the order it received it, used to order replay |
| **knowable time** | The earliest moment the platform held a value: the receive time for live capture, and the vendor's publication date or vintage for history. The point-in-time interface reads by it |
| **decision path** | A strategy's own code, parameters and configuration, and the cost model, by version. It stays fixed through a strategy's sample |
| **decision** | One run of a strategy's decision path on a session's inputs, which may ask no change |
| **request** | One change a strategy's decision asks of its target. A decision's id names its requests |
| **worst loss** | For one strategy's position, or one contract-suite run's, the sum over its legs of each leg's size times the stress move the hard caps hold, every leg moving against the position, with size counting every working and in-doubt order at its worst case, or a larger loss an exit rule fixes at a price level |
| **initial risk** | For one strategy's position, the largest worst loss journaled on an order the gate passed and the platform sent or holds in doubt, from the order that takes the position off flat to the first point where every leg is flat by the derived position and no order is working or in doubt, or for a decision scored by replay the worst loss its replay's gate computed |
| **R multiple** | A closed position's result after costs divided by its initial risk, or an open position's mark at recorded prices, net of the cost to close, divided by its initial risk so far. A before-cost figure divides the gross result by the same initial risk |
| **lone leg** | A position whose legs, by fills and the outside entries assigned to it, leave a net exposure its decision did not ask for. While a reconciliation mismatch on the position stands, its legs read as the broker reports them |
| **owner's record** | A create-only record under a prefix only the owner's role may write on every account, which holds a confirmation under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control). Its journal entry proves nothing without it |
| **close after a broker's trade** | The order execution sends for each leg still open once outside entries for trades the broker made explain the whole difference, under case 3 of rule 5 of [A fill is measured from the decision, and a position depends on a market it does not control](#a-fill-is-measured-from-the-decision-and-a-position-depends-on-a-market-it-does-not-control). No decision, override or contract-suite run stands behind it. Its **close entry** is the one journal record of that close for the position |
| **expectancy** | The mean R multiple over every position a sample opened, with one still open marked, and each decision scored as rule 4 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it) scores one |
| **hard caps** | The risk gate's limits that only the owner writes, by hand, among them the stress move and the share of the account that sizes the net cap, as the owner ruled on [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) |
| **stress move** | One leg's adverse move over at least a weekend's hold, derived from a recorded vintage and written into the hard caps by the owner, as the owner ruled on [#7](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/7) |
| **walking skeleton** | A build in which every module exists in its simplest form and the whole loop runs end to end |
| **exploratory** | A result found by looking at the data. It kills an idea or justifies a closer look, and is never a verdict |
| **registered** | A result whose hypothesis was committed in writing before the number was seen. A strategy's registered sample is the plan for judging it, committed the same way before the sample's first decision. A strategy's first registered sample runs on Paper, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-02 on [#4](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/4). A paper run with no sample open is exploratory, on the owner's ruling of 2026-11-04 there, and only a registered result promotes a strategy past Paper |

## Configuration

Machine-local config lives under `~/.config/ithildin-runtime/`. Nothing
tracked in this repository carries a secret or a machine-specific path.

No setting exists yet. Each credential this design uses gets a row here in the
change that first reads it, naming where it lives and what reads it, and a
short-lived role records what it trusts in place of where a secret lives. A credential lives in the environment of the run, in the machine's
keychain, or for the broker key in AWS Systems Manager Parameter Store under the owner's 2026-10-29 ruling on [#10](https://github.com/l3a0/ithildin-runtime/issues/10), and never in a file. The table takes the columns Setting, Secret,
Lives in, Read by and May do, and the broker's row states that it holds no
withdrawal or transfer permission and how that was checked.

| A strategy's tested largest drawdown as the worst case of a limit | Pardo sets a strategy stop-loss before trading from three factors, among them a loss of a set percentage of capital and a drawdown beyond the tested maximum or a multiple of it (EO L6026). Here no limit's worst case comes from a strategy's own record, under rule 4 of [A result is read against the luck that could have produced it](#a-result-is-read-against-the-luck-that-could-have-produced-it), and the owner writes the caps. A drawdown past the registered range may still be a registration's retiring result, which halts the strategy and is no limit, under rule 2 of [A strategy is judged over a sample](#a-strategy-is-judged-over-a-sample-and-runs-unchanged-through-it). The caps and the loss stop bound every run, including one no registration has reached. Pardo's percentage of capital is closest to the owner's loss stop, whose figure the owner writes. |
